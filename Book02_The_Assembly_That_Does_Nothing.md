# Book 2 – The Assembly That Does Nothing

## What Happened?

At dawn, Telemachus summons the people of Ithaca to their first assembly since Odysseus left twenty years earlier. Carrying a weapon and accompanied by his father’s dogs, he takes Odysseus’ seat. The elderly Aegyptius welcomes the return of public deliberation, not yet knowing that Telemachus called the meeting.

Holding the speaker’s staff, Telemachus describes the suitors’ destruction of his household and appeals to communal shame, divine justice, and sympathy. He ends in tears. The crowd pities him but remains silent. Antinous answers by blaming Penelope: for three years, she promised to choose a husband once she had woven a burial shroud for Laertes, but secretly unravelled it each night until an enslaved woman betrayed her. The suitors insist that Penelope must marry and that their consumption of Telemachus’ estate will continue until she does.

Two eagles fight above the assembly. Halitherses interprets them as a warning that Odysseus’ return and the suitors’ destruction are near; Eurymachus dismisses the omen and threatens the interpreter. Mentor then condemns not only the suitors but the silent majority who could oppose them. Leiocritus mocks the idea and dissolves the meeting. Telemachus obtains neither public support nor the ship he requested.

Alone by the sea, he prays to the god who visited him. Athena appears as Mentor, arranges a ship and crew, and tells him to prepare provisions. Telemachus secretly enlists Euryclea, who reluctantly swears not to tell Penelope. Athena puts the suitors to sleep, and the expedition departs at night. The assembly has produced no decision, but Telemachus acts anyway. Book 1 ended with a journey imagined; Book 2 ends with sailcloth filling in the wind.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 1 showed a household without effective authority. Book 2 widens the diagnosis: Ithaca is also a community whose political habits have atrophied. The fact that no assembly has met in twenty years is astonishing. Odysseus’ absence has not merely left a vacant chair; it has allowed the citizens to forget that public order is something they must practise together.

The assembly matters precisely because it fails. Telemachus does what civic life supposedly requires: he calls witnesses, names an injustice, appeals to shared norms, and asks for limited assistance. The community feels sympathy but accepts no responsibility. Homer exposes the distance between an institution’s visible form and its living function. The speaking staff, elders, speeches, omens, and audience are all present; collective courage is not.

This failure advances Telemachus’ education. In Book 1, Athena taught him to speak. Book 2 teaches him that speech is not command and moral clarity is not power. He must learn to act without confusing a refused request with a final verdict. His departure is therefore more than obedience to Athena: it is his first adaptation after public defeat.

The Book also restores Penelope to the centre through a story told against her. Antinous intends the weaving episode as an accusation, yet his complaint reveals that the supposedly powerless woman has controlled the suitors’ time for three years. Her loom has accomplished what Ithaca’s male assembly cannot: it has delayed a coercive outcome. The assembly produces words without change; the web produces change by never becoming complete.

Recent scholarship describes the assembly as a political dead end while private action proves effective. Its failure identifies the real crisis: Ithaca lacks not information but the will to turn knowledge into obligation. [The Open University summary of Elton Barker’s Book 2 commentary highlights this contrast.](https://oro.open.ac.uk/107548/)

## Major Themes

### 1. Institutions Can Survive After Civic Life Has Died

The Ithacans still know how an assembly looks. Heralds call the people; elders sit together; a staff authorises speech; one speaker answers another. But the meeting ends because Leiocritus declares it over, not because the community reaches a judgement. Procedure remains while collective agency has disappeared.

This develops Book 1’s picture of the house. There, the suitors preserved the outward form of feasting while destroying hospitality from within. Here, Ithaca preserves the outward form of deliberation while abandoning public responsibility. Homer’s pattern is unnervingly modern: institutions often decay not when their rituals vanish, but when people continue performing them after ceasing to believe they must lead to action.

The Book asks whether people remain a community when everyone privately disapproves but nobody accepts a share of the cost of opposition.

### 2. Speaking Truth Is an Act, but Not Yet Power

Telemachus’ speech is a genuine advance. In Book 1, his resistance began inside the compromised household; now he places the abuse on the public record. Even his tears contribute to the truth of the scene. They reveal both the scale of his grievance and the limits of his power.

Emily Wilson points to this moment as a challenge to readings that turn Telemachus into an ideal of masculine dominance: he admits his powerlessness and weeps before the assembled men. His growth contains vulnerability rather than replacing it. [Wilson discusses the Book 2 assembly when questioning idealised masculinity in the poem.](https://runcimanaward.org/2018/05/24/an-interview-with-emily-wilson-translator-of-homers-odyssey-part-2/)

Yet the crowd’s silence teaches a harder lesson. Truth can clarify who is responsible without making the responsible act. Modern readers may recognise the experience of reporting an abuse to a room that agrees emotionally and declines practically.

### 3. Silence Is a Political Choice

Mentor’s most radical claim is that the bystanders deserve more anger than the suitors. The suitors are at least acting consistently with their appetites and accepting, however foolishly, the risk of consequence. The many citizens possess numerical strength but sit silent.

Book 1 treated attention as the beginning of leadership: Telemachus noticed the stranger everyone else ignored. Book 2 shows attention’s limit. The Ithacans see and feel pity; what they withhold is solidarity. A tolerated injustice becomes a social arrangement.

The unsettling question is not “Why are the suitors shameless?” Their motives are obvious. It is “What has made everyone else so persuadable by their own fear?”

### 4. Control of Time Is a Form of Power

Penelope cannot openly dismiss the suitors, choose indefinite independence, or command Ithaca’s men. She can, however, make completion recede. By weaving during the day and unweaving at night, she turns domestic labour—the activity assigned to her in Book 1—into political strategy.

Her tactic creates a loop in time. Each day appears to move toward marriage; each night restores delay. As Athena used a disguised story to activate Telemachus, Penelope uses a visible artefact to make the suitors inhabit a false narrative of progress.

The web raises an enduring question: when direct refusal is unsafe, can delay become a morally legitimate form of resistance?

### 5. Inheritance Must Become Practice

Telemachus sits in his father’s seat, carries a weapon, walks with his father’s dogs, draws wine from his father’s storeroom, and sails in search of his father. These signs might make him look like a replacement Odysseus. But each also exposes the difference between possessing inherited symbols and exercising earned capacity.

He cannot control the assembly, supply his own ship, or leave without Athena’s help. Still, he prepares provisions, withstands ridicule, directs the loading, and boards. Book 1 asked whether he could imagine himself as an agent. Book 2 tests whether he can continue after humiliation.

Homer’s view of inheritance is demanding: a name can offer direction and resources, but it cannot perform courage on its bearer’s behalf.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Speaker’s Staff and the Father’s Seat

The staff gives Telemachus the recognised right to speak; the seat makes his claim of succession visible. Both are instruments of legitimate order inherited from older authority. Yet neither compels the assembly to help him. The symbols reveal legitimacy’s dependence on recognition: authority is never contained entirely in an object, office, or family name.

### Penelope’s Web

The web is a burial shroud for Laertes, so it joins future death to present courtship: Penelope claims she cannot begin a new marriage until she has prepared for the old generation’s end. Symbolically, it is also a calendar she controls. Its progress measures not cloth but political pressure.

In Book 1 the loom marked the domestic space to which Telemachus sent Penelope. Book 2 reveals that this supposedly restricted space contains a form of cunning comparable to the strategic intelligence admired in Odysseus. The motif invites us to notice that power does not always resemble command.

### The Two Eagles

The eagles descend together, fight, and tear with their talons before flying away. Halitherses reads them as a divine warning against the suitors. Eurymachus sees ordinary birds—or chooses to. The omen therefore symbolises more than fate. It dramatises interpretation itself.

Evidence does not arrive with its meaning attached. The same sight becomes warning, nonsense, or political threat according to the observer’s interests. Book 1 introduced the bird as Athena’s freedom and divine presence; Book 2 turns birds into a public test that the community fails to act upon.

### The Storeroom and Reserved Wine

Odysseus’ treasure, clothing, oil, and wine remain guarded behind strong doors. The best wine is still being kept for his possible return. The room is a material archive of hope: the household has preserved a future for a man who may be dead.

Telemachus takes the next-best wine for his voyage. He neither empties the reserve nor leaves it untouched. Symbolically, he begins converting inheritance into action while preserving a place for hope. Waiting and moving forward cease to be opposites.

### The Ship and the Fair Wind

The ship gathers many acts into one direction: a borrowed vessel, recruited rowers, measured provisions, ropes, sail, libations, and divine wind. Unlike the assembly, it is a functioning collective. Its members coordinate their bodies rather than merely agreeing that Telemachus should travel.

The wind recalls Athena’s influence in Book 1. She does not carry Telemachus to his destination. She creates favourable conditions, while human beings must still raise the mast and handle the ropes.

## Character Insights

### Telemachus

Telemachus advances and fails in the same scene. He convenes Ithaca and articulates his grievance, but frames the abuse as a private injury with public moral implications. The crowd can sympathise while telling itself that intervention remains someone else’s business.

His tears are not a retreat to childhood. The test comes afterward: he names the obstacle in prayer, accepts practical instructions, prepares supplies, withstands mockery, and departs. He is becoming less dependent on appearing powerful.

His secrecy toward Penelope remains morally complicated. He wants to spare her distress, but he also decides for her what she may know about her only son’s danger. The boy who objected to others controlling his household begins adulthood by controlling information inside it.

### Penelope

Penelope never appears in person, yet her intelligence dominates the assembly. Antinous praises her skill even while blaming her for the suitors’ conduct. This contradiction is revealing: he can admire her strategy only by treating it as provocation that excuses male retaliation.

The web creates room to manoeuvre inside constraints Penelope cannot remove. Its exposure reveals the fragility of solitary resistance: she depends on the silence of enslaved women over whom she holds power, and one betrays the scheme. Homer gives us no access to that woman’s motives. Gender solidarity is limited by hierarchy.

### Antinous

Antinous’ central technique is blame reversal. Asked why the suitors consume another man’s estate, he explains why Penelope deserves pressure. Her deception becomes justification for their ongoing exploitation, although their coercive presence created the need for deception.

He shifts rapidly from insult to false friendliness. After humiliating Telemachus, he takes his hand and invites him to feast as before. He is attempting to restore an arrangement in which Telemachus’ compliance made exploitation comfortable: punish resistance, then describe submission as peace.

### Eurymachus

Eurymachus presents himself as a rational interpreter. Birds fly everywhere, he says; not every movement carries meaning. In isolation, this scepticism could be sensible. But he accompanies it with threats against Halitherses and renewed determination to consume Telemachus’ wealth.

His reasoning is motivated disbelief. He rejects the omen because accepting it would impose a duty to change. Homer exposes the moment when scepticism becomes appetite wearing the mask of reason.

### Athena as Mentor

Athena’s help becomes increasingly material. In Book 1 she supplied courage and a plan. Here she recruits a crew, borrows a ship, induces sleep, provides wind, and accompanies Telemachus. Yet she still makes him carry provisions, face ridicule, give orders, and board the vessel.

Her use of Mentor’s appearance is fitting: the real Mentor has just spoken the truth that the community refuses to support. Athena turns his moral position into effective companionship. What Ithaca will not do collectively, divine mentorship temporarily supplies.

### Mentor, Halitherses, and the Ithacans

Mentor and Halitherses speak courageously, but neither can mobilise the crowd. Their integrity matters because it prevents silence from becoming unanimous; their impotence matters because correct speech alone does not create organisation.

The Ithacans pity Telemachus, which makes their failure more disturbing. Homer distinguishes feeling for a victim from standing with one.

### Euryclea

Euryclea responds as a nurse before she responds as a household manager. Her fear is rational: Telemachus is the family’s remaining hope, and departure exposes him to danger. Nevertheless, once bound by oath, she prepares the stores competently.

Her loyalty is both emotional and practical. She cannot decide the mission, but she makes it possible. Book 2 repeatedly contrasts spectators who feel but do nothing with helpers whose care takes material form.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Assembly and the Speaking Staff

The assembly is not a modern parliament with equal votes and formal enforcement. Elite men dominate speech; influence depends on status, lineage, reputation, and supporters. The staff marks whose turn it is to address the gathering. Telemachus can claim a hearing, but no machinery converts a persuasive case into protection.

### Marriage, Property, and Penelope’s Limited Options

Whether Penelope remains in Odysseus’ house or returns to her father involves property as well as affection. A new marriage requires negotiations over gifts, household authority, and Telemachus’ inheritance. The suitors avoid proper courtship through Icarius while enjoying Odysseus’ resources. Indecision is profitable for them.

### The Burial Shroud

Preparing a shroud for an elderly parent was a serious family duty and a public measure of honour. Penelope chooses a project the suitors cannot easily dismiss without appearing disrespectful to Laertes. Her ruse works because it makes one patriarchal obligation obstruct another.

### Omens as Public Evidence

Bird signs were not merely private superstition. Interpreters could connect unusual behaviour, direction, species, and timing to divine intention. Yet omens still required argument, as the dispute between Halitherses and Eurymachus shows. Divination did not remove politics; it became another arena within politics.

### Male Tears

Telemachus’ public weeping would not automatically disqualify him from heroic masculinity. Homeric men cry in grief, frustration, longing, and defeat. Wilson’s direct modern diction helps prevent readers from converting his maturation into emotional suppression. The question is what follows the tears, not whether they occur.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Do Not Confuse Being Heard With Being Helped

Telemachus receives attention and pity, but no ship or protection. Institutions often substitute listening for responsibility because acknowledgement is cheap. After any appeal, ask: what action, owner, and resource will follow?

### Public Failure Can Still Produce Information

The assembly reveals the field: Mentor and Halitherses will speak; the crowd will not organise; Antinous uses blame; Eurymachus uses intimidation. Failure can clarify whom to trust, what resistance costs, and which strategy must change.

### Sympathy Without Risk Preserves the Status Quo

The Ithacans’ pity lets them feel decent while remaining safe. Numbers become irrelevant when each person waits for someone else to move first. Collective courage begins when sympathy acquires a cost.

### Delay Can Be Intelligent, but It Needs an Exit

Penelope’s unweaving buys precious time. It cannot resolve the underlying crisis, and its secrecy makes it vulnerable to betrayal. Delay is wise when it protects options or prevents an irreversible harm; it becomes a trap when maintaining the delay consumes all available agency.

### Protecting Someone Is Not the Same as Excluding Them

Telemachus hides the journey to spare Penelope grief. His care is real, but so is the control embedded in deciding what she can bear. In families and teams, secrecy often presents itself as kindness. The test is whether the protected person’s welfare truly requires silence—or whether the decision-maker is avoiding a difficult conversation.

## Stoic Dialogue

### The Obstacle After the Speech

**Homeric passage:** The assembly refuses to restrain the suitors or provide the requested ship. Telemachus goes to the shore, names the obstacle in prayer, and begins another plan.

**Stoic idea:** The Stoics distinguish the quality of our action from the response it receives. A person can choose a just effort but cannot command an audience, outcome, or reputation.

**Agreement:** Telemachus does not conclude that his duty disappeared because the crowd failed him. He converts obstruction into information and continues through means still available. This resembles the Stoic discipline of returning attention to the next responsible action.

**Difference:** A Stoic would ideally ground that persistence in rational character. Homer makes it relational and divine: Athena answers the prayer, recruits the crew, and sends the wind. Telemachus’ agency is real, but it is not self-sufficient.

**Modern reflection:** Control is not the same as isolation. We can release the demand to control others while still seeking allies, systems, and favourable conditions. The mature question is not “Can I do everything myself?” but “What remains mine to do, and what help can I responsibly accept?”

### The Eagles and the Discipline of Assent

**Homeric passage:** Halitherses interprets the fighting eagles as a warning; Eurymachus dismisses them and threatens him.

**Stoic idea:** An impression is not yet a judgement. We should examine what appears before assenting to an interpretation. The Stoics also believed in a providential cosmos, so they would not automatically dismiss divination.

**Agreement:** Eurymachus is right in one narrow respect: seeing birds does not by itself prove a conclusion. Interpretation requires judgement.

**Difference:** His judgement is corrupted by interest. He does not investigate the sign; he attacks the person whose interpretation threatens his appetite. Stoic rationality requires freedom from the passions that bend evidence toward convenience.

**Modern reflection:** Scepticism can protect us from credulity, but it can also protect us from unwelcome truth. Before rejecting a warning, ask whether the evidence is weak—or whether accepting it would require a change you do not want to make.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. Where in your life has a functioning ritual concealed a failing institution?
2. When have you been heard sympathetically but left practically unsupported?
3. What would turn your concern about an injustice into solidarity with the person bearing it?
4. Which warning are you evaluating honestly, and which are you dismissing because its implications are inconvenient?
5. Where has delay protected you—and where has it become its own prison?
6. What have you inherited that now needs to become provision for a journey rather than an object of reverence?
7. When does secrecy genuinely protect another person, and when does it deny them agency?
8. Can you continue after a public failure without pretending that the failure did not hurt?

## Connections

### The Bystander Effect

Modern psychology describes how responsibility can diffuse through a group: each witness expects someone else to intervene, so the presence of many helpers may produce no help at all. Mentor’s accusation anticipates the moral core of that insight. The Ithacans are many and the suitors comparatively few, but numbers without coordinated responsibility are politically weightless.

### Margaret Atwood’s *The Penelopiad*

Atwood retells Odyssean material from Penelope’s perspective and gives special attention to the women whose labour and voices the heroic tradition marginalises. Book 2 already contains the opening for such a retelling. The famous web reaches us through Antinous, the man using Penelope’s intelligence to prosecute a case against her. Her strategy is visible; her own account of it is absent.

### Scheherazade and Survival Through Incompletion

In *One Thousand and One Nights*, Scheherazade postpones death by ensuring that a story remains unfinished at dawn. Penelope postpones forced remarriage by ensuring that a textile remains unfinished. Both transform an assigned feminine art into control over time. Neither possesses straightforward coercive power; each survives by making another person need tomorrow.

### The Word “Mentor”

The English word *mentor* descends from the name of Odysseus’ friend, but Book 2 complicates the legacy. The human Mentor offers brave criticism and achieves nothing; Athena in Mentor’s form supplies encouragement, logistics, and companionship. Effective mentorship requires both truth and enablement. Advice becomes transformative when it helps someone take the next action.

## Memorable Passage

> “As for you others I am shocked at the way in which you all sit still without even trying to stop such scandalous goings on—which you could do if you chose, for you are many and they are few.”  
> — Mentor, Samuel Butler translation

Mentor shifts the moral focus of the Book. Until this point, the obvious conflict is between Telemachus and the suitors. His accusation redraws the scene: every silent listener is now involved. The suitors’ power is revealed not as a simple fact but as an achievement continuously supplied by collective inaction.

The line matters because it refuses to treat private virtue as sufficient. The Ithacans may dislike what is happening. They may remember Odysseus fondly and pity his son. None of those feelings interrupts the feast. Homer measures civic character by whether judgement becomes coordination.

There is also a warning for Telemachus. “Many” and “few” are not merely quantities. The suitors behave as a group; the citizens remain an audience. A smaller number with shared purpose can dominate a larger number whose members experience responsibility only as a private emotion. The passage asks a question that reaches far beyond Ithaca: when wrongdoing appears powerful, how much of that power consists of everyone else remaining separate?

## Worth Arguing With

### Does Penelope’s Cunning Make Her Responsible for the Suitors’ Abuse?

Antinous’ argument is structurally familiar: because Penelope deceived the men pressuring her, their retaliation becomes her fault. But her deception did not create the coercive situation; it was a response to it. The poem allows the suitors to articulate their grievance at length, which gives the assembly genuine contest, yet their admiration for Penelope’s intelligence never extends to recognising her right not to choose them.

A modern reader should resist the assumption that clever resistance makes exploitation reciprocal. Outsmarting more powerful people does not make their power disappear.

### Is Secret, Divinely Assisted Action Too Easy an Answer to Political Failure?

The assembly fails, and Athena privately supplies what Telemachus needs. As narrative, this is satisfying. As political wisdom, it is troubling: most people cannot answer failed institutions by receiving a god, crew, ship, and wind. The Book risks making exceptional rescue seem better than the slow work of building collective courage.

Yet the contrast may be diagnostic rather than prescriptive. Athena can launch one young man; she does not heal Ithaca’s civic life. Telemachus escapes the immediate obstruction, but the community remains one in which the powerful organise and the decent watch. Personal initiative is necessary. It is not the same as public repair.

## Final Reflection

Book 2 corrects the exhilaration of finding one’s voice. Telemachus stands in his father’s place, names the wrong, risks ridicule, and asks his community to act. The result is pity, threat, and dispersal. Truth enters public space and discovers that everyone already knows enough.

Injustice is sustained by more than those who profit. It also depends upon procedures emptied of consequence, sympathetic spectators, intimidated interpreters, and the hope that someone else will go first. A community can possess all the furniture of public life and still abandon citizenship.

Telemachus’ answer is neither triumph nor despair. He weeps, prays, revises the plan, gathers provisions, accepts help, and boards. Penelope works in parallel: unable to end the pressure, she makes and unmakes the future others demand from her. Both discover forms of agency suited to constrained power.

If Homer leaves one lesson here, it may be that a refused appeal need not become a surrendered purpose. We should tell the truth publicly because silence protects the wrongdoer. But when the room will not move, we must know how to leave it, identify the faithful few, turn inheritance into provisions, and continue toward knowledge. The wind does not reward Telemachus for looking fearless. It meets him after he has learned to act while knowing exactly how powerless he has felt.
