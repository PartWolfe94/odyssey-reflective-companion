# Book 4 – The Palace Full of Ghosts

## What Happened?

Telemachus and Pisistratus arrive in Sparta during a double wedding in Menelaus’ household. A servant hesitates to admit unknown travellers, but Menelaus rebukes him: their own survival depended on hospitality. Only after the guests have been bathed and fed does Helen recognise Telemachus by his resemblance to Odysseus. Menelaus and Helen remember the absent hero, and the company weeps for those lost at Troy.

Helen pours an Egyptian drug into the wine to quiet grief. She then tells how Odysseus entered Troy disguised as a beaten beggar and how she recognised, questioned, and ultimately protected him. Menelaus answers with a less flattering memory: at the Wooden Horse, Helen imitated the voices of the warriors’ wives and nearly lured them into revealing themselves. Odysseus alone kept the men silent.

The next morning, Menelaus tells Telemachus about his own delayed return. Stranded in Egypt, he learned from the sea-goddess Eidothea how to seize her father Proteus. Menelaus and his companions hid among seals, held the shape-shifting god through a succession of terrifying forms, and forced him to speak. Proteus revealed the fates of several Greek leaders: Odysseus was alive but detained by Calypso. Menelaus offers Telemachus gifts, including horses; Telemachus tactfully declines the horses because mountainous Ithaca cannot sustain them and accepts a mixing bowl instead.

Meanwhile, the suitors discover that Telemachus has truly sailed. They prepare an ambush for his return. Penelope learns both of his secret departure and of the plot, and her anger gives way to fear. Athena sends a dream-image of Penelope’s sister to reassure her that Telemachus is protected, but the apparition refuses to say whether Odysseus lives. The Book closes not with knowledge safely gained, but with knowledge creating a new danger.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 4 brings Telemachus into the richest household he has yet seen and then reveals that wealth cannot keep grief outside. Menelaus’ palace gleams like a divine dwelling; its inhabitants speak as people still living among the dead. Troy is over geographically but active psychologically. The wedding feast, ostensibly a celebration of new households, is repeatedly overtaken by memories of households destroyed.

This is also the poem’s first sustained lesson in contested testimony. Helen and Menelaus tell adjacent stories that cannot be made fully comfortable with one another. Helen presents herself as a perceptive ally who longed for the Greek cause. Menelaus remembers her performing the voices of Greek wives outside the Wooden Horse, an act that could have destroyed everyone inside. The issue is not simply which spouse is lying. Each story establishes a version of Helen, and their marriage survives while those incompatible versions remain in the room. Scholarly readings often treat the exchange as an interpretive deadlock rather than a puzzle with a clean solution. [The Society for Classical Studies describes the two speeches as competing characterisations of Helen—ally in her own account, threat in Menelaus’.](https://classicalstudies.org/annual-meeting/155/abstract/couples-therapy-reconsideration-helens-infidelity-%C2%A0odyssey%C2%A04)

Proteus turns that problem into an image. Truth is not lying openly on the shore; it changes shape when grasped. Menelaus gains knowledge only by enduring lion, serpent, water, tree, and flame without releasing his hold. The episode is a miniature of interpretation itself: appearances multiply, disgust and fear intervene, and certainty comes only after patient contact with instability.

Finally, Book 4 completes the first phase of Telemachus’ education. He receives the fact he sought—his father was recently alive—but knowledge does not restore his household. His growth has made him visible enough to be feared. The journey that proves him capable also provokes the suitors to plan his death.

## Major Themes

### 1. Prosperity Cannot Compensate for the Irreplaceable

Menelaus possesses astonishing wealth, but he speaks of it as a poor exchange for the companions who died. His palace demonstrates the difference between replacement and restoration. Lost property can be replaced; lost time, people, and moral innocence cannot.

This sharpens the series’ picture of a damaged home. Ithaca is visibly disordered because Odysseus is absent. Sparta is outwardly ordered, yet absence governs it too. A household may function, celebrate, and shine while still being organised around grief.

What in your life looks like success from outside but feels, from within, like compensation?

### 2. Memory Is Also Self-Presentation

Helen’s story does more than remember Odysseus. It asks her listeners to remember Helen as discerning, repentant, and helpful. Menelaus’ reply gently but unmistakably reopens the charge. Neither spouse argues directly; they place narratives beside one another and let implication do the fighting.

Book 3 showed Nestor’s memory creating identity for Telemachus. Book 4 asks us to notice that every recollection also creates an identity for the speaker. We do not merely retrieve the past when we tell it. We arrange responsibility, motive, and sympathy.

The question “What happened?” is therefore inseparable from “Who do I become if this is the story?”

### 3. The Management of Grief Can Become the Avoidance of Grief

Helen’s drug prevents tears for an entire day. It is compassionate: the household has fallen into collective anguish, and Telemachus needs to hear. It is also disturbing. A chemical interruption replaces mourning with narrative before anything has been resolved.

The Greek word *pharmakon* can name a remedy, drug, or poison; context decides whether altered feeling heals or harms. Emily Wilson’s direct modern style makes Helen’s agency especially difficult to miss: she does not merely offer comfort but changes everyone’s emotional state without asking them. The episode anticipates a modern dilemma. Relief can make truth bearable, but it can also make truth easier to avoid.

The poem does not demand that suffering remain untreated. It asks whether numbness is serving life—or protecting us from a necessary encounter with loss.

### 4. Truth Requires Endurance Through Changing Appearances

Proteus becomes whatever might make Menelaus release him. The scene gives physical form to a familiar experience: just when we think we have grasped a difficult truth, it becomes anger, excuse, distraction, abstraction, or despair.

Menelaus succeeds not by defeating every form separately but by maintaining the same commitment through all of them. This is not rigidity about conclusions. It is steadiness of attention. He must hold long enough for transformation to exhaust itself and speech to begin.

Some truths are found by clever questions. Others are found by refusing to abandon the inquiry when it becomes uncomfortable.

### 5. Growth Creates Exposure

Telemachus’ departure is the first action the suitors cannot dismiss as childish performance. Their response is not respect but assassination. His development changes the political calculation: he has become a possible rival.

This revises any simple idea that courage produces immediate safety. Speaking in the assembly failed; travelling produces information but also danger. Maturity does not mean reaching a condition in which right action is protected from consequences. It means becoming able to choose while seeing more clearly what consequences may follow.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Shining Palace

Telemachus compares Menelaus’ palace to the courts of the gods. Gold, silver, bronze, ivory, and light announce successful return. Yet Menelaus immediately interprets the abundance through grief. The palace becomes a beautiful argument that material splendour and human flourishing are not the same thing.

Its brightness also makes secrecy more striking. This dazzling house contains memories that cannot be reconciled, pain that is chemically muted, and a marriage whose history remains dangerous.

### Helen’s Drugged Wine

Wine already loosens speech and joins guests in fellowship. Helen adds a substance that alters memory’s emotional force. The shared cup becomes both hospitality and control: everyone receives comfort, but no one chooses it knowingly.

In Book 3, the shared cup admitted strangers into community. Here the cup manages what the community is permitted to feel. The motif’s development asks a political question as well as a personal one: who gets to regulate the emotional atmosphere of a room?

### The Wooden Horse

The Horse holds armed men inside a false exterior. Helen’s imitation of their wives’ voices attacks the boundary between inner knowledge and outward appearance. The men must distrust what sounds most intimate.

It is an emblem of the whole Spartan episode: disguise within display, uncertain loyalties, and domestic voices entering a military machine. Odysseus survives because he can remain silent when recognition itself becomes a trap.

### Proteus’ Transformations

Lion, serpent, boar, water, tree, and fire collapse categories: animal, element, and plant become successive defences against disclosure. Proteus is truth as living resistance rather than static information.

He also mirrors Odysseus, another figure of changing forms and concealed identity. Telemachus learns about his adaptable father from a god who can be known only after his adaptations fail.

### The Horses Telemachus Refuses

Menelaus offers a magnificent gift that would be useless in rocky Ithaca. Telemachus’ refusal is courteous and practical. He can admire another kingdom without importing its status symbols into the wrong terrain.

This small decision is one of his clearest signs of growth. In Book 1, he imagined proving himself through a heroic father. Here he judges an object by whether it belongs in his actual home.

### The Dream at the Gate

Penelope’s dream-double arrives with comfort but withholds the answer she most desires. The dream stands between revelation and silence, just as Penelope stands between hope and mourning.

Divine care has limits. Athena protects Telemachus and eases Penelope’s panic, but she does not give Penelope control over the future—or even full knowledge of the present.

## Character Insights

### Telemachus

Telemachus is no longer merely coached through etiquette. He compares evidence, asks a precise question, survives emotionally charged stories, and declines an unsuitable gift without insulting its giver. His judgement is becoming local and practical rather than theatrical.

Yet he remains largely unaware of the danger his independence has created. The Book divides his experience from Penelope’s so that the reader sees a cost he cannot. Becoming adult includes leaving parental protection; it also exposes how much anxiety that protection had been containing.

### Helen

Helen is perceptive, commanding, eloquent, and impossible to reduce to either victim or traitor. She recognises Telemachus first, controls the household’s mood, and narrates her own place in the Trojan War. Her story claims repentance and restored allegiance.

But Menelaus’ story prevents her self-portrait from becoming definitive. Was she deliberately testing the Horse for Troy, compelled by a god, divided against herself, or performing some more complicated loyalty? Homer preserves the ambiguity. Helen’s intelligence gives her unusual power over interpretation, but not complete ownership of it.

### Menelaus

Menelaus has returned with wealth, a wife, and knowledge, yet speaks like a survivor for whom success feels morally embarrassing. He is generous to strangers because he remembers dependence. His hospitality is gratitude converted into policy.

He is also a subtle storyteller. He does not openly contradict Helen; he follows her account with one that changes its meaning. His marriage appears stable enough to contain disagreement but perhaps not honest enough to name it directly.

### Pisistratus

Pisistratus again shows emotional intelligence beyond his years. When the household weeps, he recalls his own dead brother but suggests that lament should not consume the evening. His intervention prepares the ground for Helen’s drug while offering a less invasive form of regulation: grief acknowledged, then bounded by occasion.

He is a valuable contrast to Telemachus. Both are sons shaped by war, but Pisistratus grew within a functioning family that taught him how sorrow and ceremony can coexist.

### Penelope

Penelope learns that Euryclea helped conceal Telemachus’ departure. Her grief contains more than maternal fear: decisions about her own household have been made around her in the name of protecting her. She is queen and yet excluded from knowledge.

The dream offers reassurance without agency. Penelope can pray, wait, and trust, but cannot pursue. Telemachus’ coming-of-age thus has an asymmetrical cost: his mobility is purchased partly through his mother’s enforced helplessness.

### The Suitors

The suitors’ ambush exposes what their earlier mockery concealed. They did not regard Telemachus as harmless because they rejected violence; they regarded him as harmless because they thought him weak. Once he acts independently, they escalate from consumption to planned murder.

Their crime is not only bad hospitality. It is a politics that tolerates the young only while the young remain incapable of succession.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Sparta After Troy

Menelaus and Helen’s household belongs to the tradition of the Greek *nostoi*, the many returns from Troy. Ancient audiences knew that “homecoming” could mean shipwreck, murder, displacement, or uneasy restoration. The domestic splendour of Sparta therefore carries the pressure of alternative endings.

### Egyptian Knowledge and *Pharmaka*

Egypt appears as a place of wealth, old religious knowledge, and powerful drugs. Greek literature often used Egypt as a near yet marvellous culture whose expertise exceeded ordinary Greek practice. Helen’s acquisition of the drug during Menelaus’ wanderings makes foreign knowledge both valuable and morally ambiguous.

### Marriage as Alliance and Exchange

The double wedding joins households through carefully arranged marriages and gifts. Menelaus’ family celebration shows marriage as a public institution before it is a private romance. This makes Helen and Menelaus’ own contested history an unsettling background: the household is reproducing the system whose catastrophic failure began the war.

### Enslaved Labour and Palace Splendour

Servants wash guests, prepare food, tend animals, and carry out commands. The palace’s beauty depends on labour performed by people with little freedom, many likely acquired through war or raiding. Wilson’s translation consistently uses direct terms such as “slave” where older translations often soften status into “maid” or “servant.” That difference matters: hospitality for elite guests is made possible by coercion elsewhere in the household.

### Proteus and the Old Man of the Sea

Proteus belongs to a widespread mythic pattern: a supernatural being withholds knowledge and changes shape to escape capture. Menelaus’ success requires ritual instruction from Eidothea, physical endurance, and a correctly timed question. Knowledge is relational; even a king cannot simply command nature to disclose itself.

### Elysium and Unequal Afterlives

Proteus predicts that Menelaus will not experience ordinary death but will reach Elysium because he is married to Helen, Zeus’ daughter. The reward is based less on moral excellence than divine kinship. Homeric afterlife is not yet a consistent system in which virtue receives proportionate reward.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Do Not Confuse Compensation with Healing

Achievement, possessions, and new experiences can make life larger after loss. They cannot make the lost thing interchangeable. Healing begins when we stop asking success to perform resurrection.

### Listen for the Self-Portrait Inside Every Story

When people describe a disputed past, notice not only what they say happened but what role the account gives them: rescuer, victim, innocent observer, reluctant participant. This need not make the story false. It makes listening more exact.

### Relief Should Restore Agency, Not Replace It

Pain relief, distraction, medication, ritual, and rest may be humane necessities. Helen’s wine becomes troubling because the recipients neither know nor consent. Care is most trustworthy when the sufferer remains a participant in deciding what happens to consciousness.

### Hold the Question Through Its Protean Forms

A difficult conversation may turn into anger; a project may turn into boredom; grief may turn into busyness. Do not mistake a change of form for the disappearance of the underlying issue.

### Decline Gifts That Do Not Fit Your Terrain

Prestige often arrives packaged as opportunity: a role, lifestyle, technology, or symbol admired elsewhere. Telemachus’ horses are a reminder to ask whether the gift can live in the landscape of your values and obligations.

### Expect Growth to Alter Other People’s Behaviour

When you stop playing an assigned role, systems built around your passivity may resist. Resistance is not proof that development is mistaken; it may reveal what your former smallness was protecting.

## Stoic Dialogue

### Grief, Wealth, and What Cannot Be Replaced

**Homeric passage:** Menelaus says he would surrender much of his wealth if doing so could restore the companions killed at Troy.

**Stoic idea:** Wealth is a “preferred indifferent”: useful but incapable of making a life good. Virtue, not possession, determines flourishing.

**Agreement:** Menelaus has learned that abundance cannot answer mortality. The palace may improve comfort but cannot settle whether its owner is living wisely or well.

**Difference:** His grief remains attached to an impossible exchange—goods for lives. A Stoic might urge him to honour the dead through present character rather than mentally bargaining with fate. Homer is more willing to let unresolved longing remain part of love.

**Modern reflection:** We need not stop valuing comfort to stop asking it for salvation. The right question is not whether wealth matters, but what kind of loss it is structurally unable to repair.

### Holding Proteus

**Homeric passage:** Menelaus keeps hold of Proteus while the god changes into frightening animals, elements, and fire.

**Stoic idea:** First impressions are not commands. The disciplined mind pauses, examines what appears, and refuses assent to fear merely because fear arrives vividly.

**Agreement:** Menelaus succeeds by not allowing each appearance to dictate a new intention. Constancy makes knowledge possible.

**Difference:** Stoicism normally directs this discipline inward, toward judgements. Homer dramatises it as physical struggle against an external divine being. The myth preserves an important danger: persistence can become coercion if the object held is another person rather than one’s own attention.

**Modern reflection:** Hold to the inquiry, not necessarily to your first answer. Steadiness of purpose and flexibility of conclusion belong together.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. What apparent success in your life has functioned more as compensation than fulfilment?
2. When you tell a painful story, what version of yourself does the telling protect?
3. Which competing memories in a family or relationship may both contain truth?
4. When does emotional relief help you return to life, and when does it postpone necessary grief?
5. What difficult truth keeps changing form whenever you try to face it?
6. Which impressive gift or opportunity would be useless on the terrain of your actual life?
7. Who benefits when you remain less capable, less informed, or less independent?
8. What reassurance are you demanding from life that no loving person can honestly give?

## Connections

### Edgar Allan Poe’s “The Raven” and Nepenthe

Poe’s grieving speaker longs for “nepenthe,” the legendary substance associated with Helen’s drug. In both works, oblivion appears as a mercy that cannot restore the lost beloved. The desire is understandable precisely because memory has become unbearable. Homer’s scene is stranger, however: Helen administers forgetfulness socially, turning private anguish into a managed evening.

### Conflicting Testimony in *Rashomon*

Akira Kurosawa’s *Rashomon* makes a disputed event available only through self-interested accounts. Helen and Menelaus anticipate the same interpretive unease. Testimony may reveal genuine experience while also arranging shame and dignity. The mature question is not always “Which speaker can I eliminate?” but “What does each version illuminate and conceal?”

### Proteus in Later Literature

“Protean” now means capable of continual transformation. Writers from Shakespeare onward use Proteus to represent changeability, theatrical identity, and elusive reality. Homer’s original image adds a neglected element: transformation is defensive. Change can be creativity, but it can also be a strategy for avoiding accountability.

### Trauma and the Successful Survivor

Menelaus resembles people who return from collective catastrophe to lives others envy. Survival may bring gratitude, guilt, intrusive memory, and discomfort with privilege at once. His palace warns against assuming that visible recovery measures inner restoration.

## Memorable Passage

> “Would that I had only a third of what I now have so that I had stayed at home, and all those were living who perished on the plain of Troy, far from Argos.”  
> — Menelaus, Samuel Butler translation

Menelaus speaks from inside the fantasy that prosperity closes history. His wealth is evidence that he returned successfully, but every object also becomes evidence that others did not return. The palace is therefore both reward and accusation.

His arithmetic is revealing. He tries to express grief as an exchange—two thirds of the wealth for the dead—while knowing no market can make the trade. Quantification marks the point at which value defeats price. The lives are beyond calculation, yet the bereaved mind calculates because it cannot stop imagining reversal.

The passage also complicates envy. Telemachus sees divine radiance; Menelaus experiences survivor’s surplus. We often compare our interior lack to another person’s exterior abundance. Homer asks us to imagine the ghosts by which abundance may be measured from within.

## Worth Arguing With

### Is Helen Caring for the Mourners—or Controlling Them?

The drug permits conversation to continue and may prevent a young guest’s visit from collapsing into grief. Yet Helen alters everyone’s feelings without consent, then uses the calm to tell a story favourable to herself. Care and narrative control become difficult to separate.

A modern reader should resist two easy verdicts: that all emotional medication is avoidance, or that relief is automatically benevolent. The ethical question concerns knowledge, consent, purpose, and whether feeling can return when it has something still to teach.

### Does the Poem Reward Birth More Than Virtue?

Proteus promises Menelaus Elysium because he is Zeus’ son-in-law. The prediction sits awkwardly beside Menelaus’ genuine hospitality and grief: he may deserve admiration, but the stated reason for privilege is connection.

Homer’s divine order is often aristocratic rather than morally equal. Instead of translating it too quickly into a just universe, we can let it expose a durable human habit—describing advantages of birth, marriage, and access as though they were rewards for character.

### Is Protective Secrecy Still a Form of Disrespect?

Euryclea keeps Telemachus’ departure from Penelope because he ordered it and because disclosure would cause pain. The secrecy may be understandable, but it leaves Penelope powerless in her own home. Protection becomes indistinguishable from deciding that another adult cannot be trusted with reality.

Book 4 asks whether love can reduce suffering without reducing the loved person’s agency. Its answer remains intentionally uncomfortable.

## Final Reflection

Book 4 is filled with people trying to live beside truths they cannot master. Menelaus surrounds loss with treasure. Helen quiets grief and narrates herself toward innocence. A sea-god becomes fire and water rather than submit to questioning. Penelope receives comfort but not the answer on which comfort seems to depend.

Telemachus moves through these scenes as an apprentice in interpretation. He learns that a beautiful household may be haunted, that witnesses can remember the same war into different moral worlds, and that information must sometimes be held through frightening transformations. Most importantly, he learns without yet realising that knowledge changes the knower’s position. His journey makes Odysseus more real to him—and makes him more dangerous to the suitors.

The Book’s wisdom is not that truth eventually becomes simple. Proteus speaks, but Helen remains ambiguous; Odysseus is alive, but inaccessible; Telemachus is growing, but now hunted. Truth offers orientation rather than immunity.

Perhaps that is why Sparta shines so intensely. Homer lets splendour attract the eye, then teaches us to look for the grief, labour, contradiction, and unfinished history inside it. To see clearly is not to strip the world of beauty. It is to recognise how much reality beauty can contain without resolving.
