# Book 9 – The Name He Could Not Keep

## What Happened?

Odysseus finally answers Alcinous’ question. He names himself, describes Ithaca as the place he loves above every richer home, and begins telling the Phaeacians what happened after Troy.

His first stop was Ismarus, where he and his men sacked the city of the Cicones, killed its men, enslaved its women, and divided the plunder. Odysseus urged an immediate departure, but his crew stayed to feast. Ciconian reinforcements arrived, killing six men from every ship. Storms then drove the fleet off course to the Lotus-Eaters, whose food dissolved the desire for home. Odysseus dragged the affected men back in tears and tied them beneath the benches.

The fleet next reached the country of the Cyclopes. Leaving most ships at a nearby island, Odysseus took twelve men to investigate. They entered Polyphemus’ cave while he was away. Although the crew advised stealing food and leaving, Odysseus waited, hoping to receive a guest gift. Polyphemus returned, sealed the entrance, rejected the authority of Zeus, and ate six men over three meals.

Unable to kill him without trapping everyone behind the stone, Odysseus devised another plan. He intoxicated Polyphemus with Maron’s powerful wine, called himself “Nobody,” and drove a fire-hardened olive stake into the Cyclops’ eye. When Polyphemus cried that Nobody was attacking him, his neighbours withdrew. The survivors escaped beneath his rams.

Once at sea, Odysseus taunted the blinded Cyclops. His crew begged him to stop, but he revealed his real name, father, and home. Polyphemus then prayed to his father Poseidon that Odysseus return late, alone, and to trouble. The men escaped another thrown rock, divided the stolen flock, and sacrificed; Zeus rejected Odysseus’ offering. They sailed on, relieved to live and grieving those lost.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 9 is where the poem’s hero becomes its narrator—and where his account immediately complicates the hero created by song. In Book 8, Demodocus sang Odysseus from the outside. Now Odysseus takes the bard’s place and tells what only he can tell. His intelligence is dazzling. So is his ability to arrange events into a story about that intelligence.

This matters because no neutral camera accompanies him. He is speaking to wealthy sailors whose hospitality and transport he needs. He presents himself as the famous king of Ithaca, a commander frequently disobeyed, a respecter of priests, an ingenious survivor, and a man punished by forces larger than himself. All of this may be true. It is also excellent self-presentation. The [Oxford Critical Guide’s account of Book 9](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780191926846.003.0010) emphasises both his narrative skill and the curiosity and appetite for goods that make his leadership morally uncertain.

The sequence of episodes asks what can make a person lose home. The Lotus-Eaters erase the wish to return. Polyphemus threatens the body that must return. But Odysseus’ own hunger for recognition creates the most consequential danger. “Nobody” escapes; “Odysseus” calls the curse down upon himself. The obstacle is no longer simply hostile nature or divine anger. It is a heroic identity unable to let a victory remain anonymous.

Book 9 also prevents the captive-woman simile in Book 8 from becoming easy compassion. There Odysseus wept like a woman beaten into slavery after her city fell. Here his first deed after Troy is to destroy another city and take its women. Homer places identification with the victim beside participation in the machinery that creates victims. Suffering may enlarge sympathy, but it does not automatically confer innocence.

Finally, the Cyclops episode tests civilisation rather than merely contrasting it with savagery. The boundary between human and monster remains real, but it no longer runs neatly between two bodies.

## Major Themes

### 1. The Self That Saves Us May Not Be the Self That Wants Credit

Odysseus survives by becoming “Nobody.” The false name is not only a pun; it is a temporary release from rank, ancestry, reputation, and the demand to perform a heroic self. As Nobody, he observes, waits, collaborates, and thinks.

Once safe, he reverses the achievement. He attaches his name to the wound so that the deed can become fame. The announcement feels like victory, but it gives Polyphemus exactly what he needs to direct his prayer. A name turns an injury into a relationship of vengeance.

Book 8 asked how a public story can miss a private person. Book 9 asks the inverse question: why might a person endanger himself to enter the public story? We often say that ego makes people exaggerate. Homer notices something subtler: ego can make us disclose what wisdom taught us to conceal.

### 2. Home Depends on Memory—and Memory Can Hurt

The Lotus-Eaters do not torture, imprison, or threaten. They offer food. Their danger is the end of wanting: the men no longer care to report back or return home.

This develops Calypso’s temptation in Book 5. Calypso offered a magnificent alternative life; the lotus removes the capacity to prefer any life at all. Odysseus forces the weeping men aboard because their present desire has been altered by something that destroys their enduring commitments.

The scene makes homecoming psychological. Ithaca exists only as a destination while it remains present in memory. To love home is therefore to accept longing, frustration, and grief. Not every relief from pain is liberation; some relief works by erasing the self for whom anything mattered.

### 3. Civilisation Is Cooperation, Not Comfort

The Cyclopes have abundant food without organised agriculture. Each man rules his own cave and ignores his neighbours. They possess households but not a shared world: no assemblies, shipbuilders, laws, or common projects.

Polyphemus’ neighbours come when he cries out, so their isolation is not absolute. Yet the Nobody trick succeeds because their cooperation is thin. They ask one question, accept a literal answer, and return home. By contrast, Odysseus’ plan requires several men to shape, heat, lift, and turn the stake together.

Homer’s distinction is not simply refined versus primitive. Polyphemus knows animal husbandry and affection. The Greeks know councils and ships but also raiding and slavery. Civilisation appears as a fragile practice of restraint, reciprocity, shared deliberation, and coordinated effort—not a possession one culture can permanently claim.

### 4. Leadership Is Tested by What Happens After Good Advice

At Ismarus, Odysseus gives the right order and his men ignore it. At the Cyclops’ cave, his men give the right advice and he ignores them. The parallel prevents him from monopolising foresight.

His leadership ranges from lifesaving to catastrophic. He forcibly rescues the lotus-eaters, recognises that killing the sleeping Cyclops would doom everyone, invents a collective escape, and silences grief until the ship is moving. He also leads twelve men into an avoidable trap and disregards unanimous pleas not to taunt Polyphemus.

The Book refuses the comforting idea that a leader is either competent or incompetent. The same confidence that produces action under pressure can resist correction. A group’s survival depends not only on whether the leader can command, but on whether authority remains permeable to truth from below.

### 5. Hospitality Can Become Entitlement

Polyphemus commits the Book’s grossest violation of *xenia*: he eats the strangers who appeal to Zeus’ protection. His promised “guest gift”—eating Odysseus last—is a hideous parody of the institution.

But Odysseus does not arrive empty of aggression. He enters an absent stranger’s cave, eats his cheese, and waits because he expects a present. His crew’s proposed alternative is theft. The Cyclops’ first question—are these visitors traders or pirates?—is perceptive, especially after the raid on Ismarus.

Book 7 showed hospitality as protection for the vulnerable stranger. Book 9 asks what happens when a powerful stranger invokes that protection while treating access to another’s household as his right. Customs designed to restrain violence can themselves become a language of demand.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Lotus

The lotus is pleasure without narrative. It does not persuade the sailors that another home is better; it makes the categories of journey and home irrelevant.

Food elsewhere establishes relationship and restores identity. This food severs both. The men stop wanting even to carry news back to their companions. The lotus therefore symbolises less ordinary indulgence than the wish to be released from memory, obligation, and the effort of continuing.

### Maron’s Wine

The wine comes from a small act of restraint inside a violent raid. Odysseus spared Maron, Apollo’s priest, along with his wife and child; Maron answered with gifts. A remnant of respected hospitality survives the destruction of Ismarus and later becomes the weapon that defeats hospitality’s worst violator.

Wine also represents culture: cultivation, patience, proportion, ritual, and sharing. Polyphemus drinks it unmixed and without restraint. Yet the Greeks use this civilised product to incapacitate and mutilate him. Culture is power, not automatic goodness.

### The Olive Stake

The green olive trunk begins as Polyphemus’ future staff. Odysseus cuts, smooths, points, chars, and coordinates it into a weapon. Raw material becomes technology through foresight and collective labour.

The olive has already sheltered Odysseus in Book 5 and carries associations of endurance and Athena’s intelligent order. Here protection becomes violence. The image refuses to separate ingenuity from its capacity to injure.

### The Single Eye

Polyphemus’ eye is literally the point of vulnerability. Symbolically, one-eyedness suggests a world with no second perspective: appetite, household, and grievance fill the whole field.

But Odysseus becomes figuratively one-eyed after the escape. He sees the glorious authorship of the victory and cannot see the rock, curse, or terror his announcement may cause. Homer does not let the symbol belong safely to the monster.

### The Cave and the Stone

The cave is shelter turned prison. Its contents—milk, lambs, baskets, fire—suggest domestic order, while the stone gives one master absolute control over entry and exit.

Escape requires something like a second birth: in darkness the men endure terror, then emerge beneath living bodies into morning. [The Dickinson commentary notes the episode’s pattern of confinement, pain, and re-emergence](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/intro/essays-book-9), but the rebirth is morally incomplete. Odysseus comes out alive, not transformed enough to govern his pride.

### The Rams

The rams reverse ordinary dominance. The men who arrived as armed masters survive as quiet burdens beneath animals. Strength is no longer standing upright and being seen; it is holding patiently to wool.

Polyphemus’ speech to his favourite ram reveals tenderness inside the cannibal. His empathy is real but radically bounded: he can imagine his animal mourning him while remaining unable to recognise strangers as beings whose deaths matter. Affection for one’s own is not yet ethics.

### The Sea and the Thrown Rocks

The sea has represented uncertainty, separation, and passage. Here Polyphemus weaponises it indirectly. Each rock makes the water carry the ship—first back towards danger, then away.

Odysseus’ voice travels across the same space. Distance should provide safety, but sound reopens contact. The sea cannot protect a person who keeps calling danger back to himself.

## Character Insights

### Odysseus

This is Odysseus at his most impressive and most culpable. He can postpone revenge when immediate killing would trap everyone. He studies routines, uses available materials, distributes tasks, invents a name with two meanings, and waits beneath the ram in pain and fear. His courage is intellectual as much as physical.

Yet two admissions expose his judgement. He says his crew’s plan to leave the cave would have been better. He also records their pleas for silence before he names himself. Because this is his own account, these details are extraordinary. He may include them to display honesty, explain divine hostility, or dramatise how overpowering heroic anger can be. Whatever his motive, he does not edit his worst decisions away.

At the opening he declares that nothing is sweeter than one’s homeland. At the climax he jeopardises homecoming for reputation. His deepest value and his strongest habit are at war.

### Polyphemus

Polyphemus is not misunderstood into monstrosity. He murders, dismembers, and eats defenceless strangers; rejects any law above his appetite; and takes pleasure in a cruel counterfeit gift. His blindness does not create his moral isolation.

But Homer gives him features that resist caricature. His dairy is organised. He pairs young animals with their mothers. He anticipates seasonal work. He loves his lead ram. He remembers prophecy and calls neighbouring Cyclopes friends. He is not nature without culture; he is competence and attachment without reciprocal moral limits.

His expectation that the prophesied Odysseus would be a gigantic warrior is also revealing. Like Euryalus in Book 8, he mistakes visible size for human significance. He is defeated partly by his inability to imagine intelligence arriving in an unimpressive body.

### The Crew

The crew are neither disposable fools nor reliable victims. At Ismarus they ignore a sound command because victory has become appetite. At the cave they perceive danger more accurately than their captain. With the stake they act bravely and in coordination; during the taunts they understand the risk while Odysseus does not.

Their six deaths inside the cave arise from Polyphemus’ evil, but the expedition itself arose from Odysseus’ choice. Book 9 quietly asks what subordinates owe a leader—and what a leader owes people who bear the consequences of his curiosity.

### The Lotus-Eaters

The Lotus-Eaters cause no deliberate harm. They greet strangers with their own food, a distorted mirror of good hospitality. The danger lies in a mismatch between intention and effect: a generous gift can destroy the recipient’s capacity to leave.

They represent no villain to defeat, only a life incompatible with purposeful return. Homer’s gentlest people may therefore pose the purest threat to the poem’s central value.

### The Cicones

The Cicones appear first as targets and then as defenders. Odysseus compresses the initial sack into a few efficient lines; their organised counterattack receives more narrative space. The people he treats as plunder reveal skill, solidarity, and the power to answer violence.

Their women never speak. That silence is morally important, especially after Book 8’s captive-woman simile. The story counts male casualties ship by ship but treats enslaved women as divided wealth. What a narrator itemises tells us whose losses his world has learned to see.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Raiding Was Familiar; That Does Not Make It Neutral

Early Greek epic inhabits a warrior culture in which maritime raiding, plunder, and enslavement could produce wealth and status. Polyphemus’ question about whether the strangers are traders or raiders is the same conventional question Nestor asked Telemachus in Book 3. Piracy was a recognisable occupation, not an unimaginable aberration.

Modern readers should neither pretend the poem shares our moral vocabulary nor let historical familiarity erase victims. Wilson’s translation uses direct terms for enslavement and foregrounds the Book as an encounter between an armed outsider and an indigenous inhabitant. In an [interview about translating Book 9](https://web.sas.upenn.edu/discentes/2020/08/24/blm-x-clst-a-series-of-interviews-with-the-faculty-of-penn-classics-part-1-professor-emily-wilson/), she explains that her title identifies the visitor as a pirate and the inhabitant as a shepherd. That framing prevents readers from seeing only through the Greek invader’s gaze. It does not excuse cannibalism; it restores the question of who entered whose home.

### *Xenia* Is Mutual Restraint

Guest-friendship protects travellers in a world without inns, passports, or reliable public security. Hosts provide food, shelter, information, and gifts; guests respect the household and do not abuse protection. Zeus oversees the relationship because strangers are unusually exposed to power.

Polyphemus violates the host’s obligations absolutely. Odysseus tests the guest’s side of the institution. His expectation of a gift is culturally intelligible, but entering, eating, and waiting in an absent owner’s cave puts entitlement inside the appeal to custom.

### Household and Assembly

The Cyclopes live in separate households, each patriarch ruling wife and children without common assembly. For Homer’s audience, public deliberation, law, agriculture, shipbuilding, and exchange distinguish a community from isolated domestic units.

The Phaeacians provide the immediate contrast: they assemble, allocate labour, build ships, hear disputes, feast collectively, and revise plans in response to a stranger. Civilisation is pictured as the capacity to organise beyond kin.

### Goat Island and the Settler’s Eye

Odysseus describes the neighbouring island almost as a prospectus: deep soil, vines, water, harbour, pasture, and no cultivation. Scholarship has long connected this passage with the conditions imagined by archaic Greek colonisation; [Alfonso Mele identifies the description as a model of a site viewed for settlement](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/ElAnt/V11N1/mele.html).

The language is revealing. Land appears “unused” because its inhabitants do not exploit it as Greek sailors would. A landscape can be genuinely fertile and still not be waiting for the first outsider who can imagine extracting more from it.

### Wine and Measure

Greeks normally diluted wine with water. Maron’s vintage is so concentrated that it requires exceptional dilution. Polyphemus drinks repeated cups of it neat, turning hospitality’s shared and measured drink into solitary excess.

His error is not simply that he drinks alcohol. He accepts a foreign substance without understanding its strength, trusts appetite over proportion, and allows the guest he tried to dominate to set the terms of exchange.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Do Not Trade Safety for the Satisfaction of Being Recognised

There are moments when anonymity, silence, or letting someone misunderstand you is not cowardice but discipline. A victory does not become less real because the defeated person never learns your name.

### Build a Life You Must Remember to Want

The lotus asks an uncomfortable question: if pain vanished only because purpose vanished with it, would that be healing? Commitments often survive through deliberate remembrance, especially when immediate feeling no longer supports them.

### Invite Dissent Before Crisis

Odysseus’ crew sees two dangers he misses: waiting in the cave and shouting from the ship. Courage at the top is not enough if useful fear cannot alter the decision.

### Notice When Curiosity Spends Other People’s Risk

Exploration is admirable when the explorer bears the cost. It becomes morally complicated when subordinates must enter the cave too. Leaders should ask who pays for an experiment that satisfies their own need to know.

### Do Not Mistake Care for One’s Own as Moral Wholeness

Polyphemus loves his ram and devours strangers. Tenderness inside a private circle is good, but character is tested at its boundaries—especially by people who offer no advantage and possess no claim except vulnerability.

### Keep Tools and Virtues Distinct

Wine, craft, rhetoric, and intelligence defeat brute force. They do not automatically make their user just. Skill answers “Can this be done?”; ethics still has to answer “Should it?”

## Stoic Dialogue

### The Name Shouted Across the Water

**Homeric passage:** Safely offshore, Odysseus ignores his crew and identifies himself to Polyphemus, who then curses his return.

**Stoic idea:** Epictetus repeatedly distinguishes our character and choices from externals such as fame, status, and another person’s opinion. Freedom requires refusing to let externals command action.

**Agreement:** The situation is already won. Odysseus needs nothing from Polyphemus except to leave. His craving for acknowledgement converts an external—being known as the victor—into a master of his conduct.

**Difference:** Homeric heroism needs deeds to be attached to names so that *kleos*, lasting renown, can exist. A Stoic could let the act disappear from public memory if justice and survival required it. Homer understands the allure of glory too deeply to dismiss it, even while showing its cost.

**Modern reflection:** Before correcting a hostile person’s view of you, ask whether the correction serves a duty or merely relieves the ache of being unseen.

### Dragging the Lotus-Eaters Back

**Homeric passage:** Odysseus forces the affected sailors aboard while they weep and binds them beneath the benches.

**Stoic idea:** Stoic freedom is not doing whatever one presently desires; it is governing desire through reason in accordance with one’s considered commitments and roles.

**Agreement:** The lotus has not revealed the men’s authentic preference. It has disabled memory of the project they chose together. Odysseus protects their longer agency against an altered impulse.

**Difference:** Stoicism aims at self-government, whereas Odysseus imposes another person’s prior values by force. The urgency may justify his act, but it leaves a genuine problem: who may decide that someone’s current desire is not truly theirs?

**Modern reflection:** Care sometimes requires refusing to cooperate with a person’s destructive impulse. That responsibility should make us more humble, not more certain of our right to control.

### Revenge Inside the Cave

**Homeric passage:** Odysseus initially reaches for his sword, then recognises that killing Polyphemus would leave everyone sealed inside.

**Stoic idea:** An overwhelming first impulse is not yet a moral failure; wisdom lies in withholding assent long enough to judge consequences.

**Agreement:** Odysseus feels rage and does not obey it. Practical reason turns revenge into a rescue plan. This is self-command under extreme pressure.

**Difference:** He later describes the scheme partly as a means to win glory and punishment, not merely survival. Stoicism would ask whether the desire for revenge remains hidden inside the more rational strategy.

**Modern reflection:** The fact that an action is clever and delayed does not prove that anger no longer governs it. Sometimes impulse learns to make plans.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. When has the need to receive credit made a completed victory less secure?
2. Which painful memory keeps an important commitment alive for you?
3. Where might comfort be weakening desire rather than satisfying it?
4. Whose risk pays for your curiosity, ambition, or need for experience?
5. When have people below you understood a danger you were too invested to see?
6. How far does your tenderness extend beyond family, team, or tribe?
7. Which customs do you invoke as principles but sometimes practise as entitlements?
8. What does your own version of events count carefully—and whose cost does it compress?

## Connections

### Dante’s Ulysses

In *Inferno* 26, Dante transforms Ulysses into a voyager whose hunger for knowledge outruns home, family, and moral limit. The seeds are already here. Odysseus says Ithaca is dearest, yet enters the cave because he wants to see what kind of people live there. Curiosity is one of his great powers and one of the appetites for which others pay.

### Joyce’s “Cyclops”

The “Cyclops” episode of James Joyce’s *Ulysses* is governed by a loud nationalist narrator whose vision contracts the world into insiders and outsiders. Joyce relocates one-eyedness from anatomy to ideology: the inability to see a person except through a totalising group story. Homer prepares that move by giving both Polyphemus and Odysseus morally narrowed fields of vision.

### The Lotus in Tennyson

Tennyson’s poem “The Lotos-Eaters” expands the brief Homeric episode into a collective argument for rest. His sailors ask why human beings should keep labouring when everything in nature seems able simply to remain. The adaptation reveals what Homer leaves compressed: weariness can make oblivion sound philosophical.

### Colonial Travel Writing

Odysseus’ inventory of Goat Island anticipates a recurrent language of empire: land is described as empty or wasted when local people do not farm, trade, settle, or organise it in the observer’s preferred way. The description may be practically accurate and politically interested at once. Seeing resources is never only seeing; it also imagines who ought to control them.

### Addiction, Compulsion, and the Divided Will

The lotus episode resembles modern experiences in which immediate desire separates from long-term identity. The comparison should not become a diagnosis: Homer offers no clinical theory. What he grasps is the divided will. A person may fiercely resist the very rescue that restores the values by which they once chose to live.

## Memorable Passage

> “Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.”
> — Samuel Butler translation

The sentence gives Polyphemus everything that “Nobody” had withheld: deed, name, father, and address. It is almost a miniature heroic poem, attaching action to lineage and homeland so that fame can travel accurately.

That completeness is precisely why it is disastrous. Odysseus does not merely boast; he supplies the information required for consequence. The statement converts a solved problem into a lasting one.

Its irony reaches back to the opening of the Book. Odysseus tells the Phaeacians his name so that they may form a bond and someday be welcomed in Ithaca. Naming creates reciprocal hospitality there. He tells Polyphemus the same name to make humiliation permanent. Identity is not good or dangerous by itself; its wisdom depends on audience, timing, and purpose.

The line also exposes the hero’s division. “Who lives in Ithaca” is how he defines himself, but saying it delays his return to Ithaca. He sacrifices the thing he loves to the need to be recognised as the man who loves it.

## Worth Arguing With

### Is Odysseus a Civilised Guest—or an Armed Intruder?

Polyphemus’ cannibalism cannot be softened into legitimate self-defence. Yet the familiar fairy-tale framing can prevent us from asking why Odysseus entered an absent shepherd’s cave, consumed his food, rejected his crew’s advice, and expected a gift.

Emily Wilson’s emphasis on the encounter’s colonial structure corrects the camera angle without reversing the moral verdict. Two facts can coexist: Polyphemus commits an atrocity, and Odysseus approached his household with assumptions formed by a raiding culture that regarded foreign wealth as available. Moral clarity about one person’s violence should not erase the other’s entitlement.

### Does Saving Someone Justify Overriding Them?

Odysseus drags the lotus-eaters back as they cry. Most readers accept the act because the food has altered their desires and immediate departure is essential. But the scene offers no consent procedure and no later testimony from the rescued men.

The dilemma remains modern: addiction, coercive control, psychiatric crisis, and manipulative systems can impair choice, while claims of “saving” others can rationalise domination. The Book gives an urgent case for intervention, not a general licence. The harder wisdom is that respecting agency and protecting agency may sometimes point in opposite directions.

### Whose Death Counts as Leadership Failure?

Odysseus blames his men for staying at Ismarus, fairly enough. He admits his own error at the cave. But the narration quickly turns six eaten companions into the occasion for his greatest intellectual triumph.

Heroic storytelling naturally centres the survivor whose ingenuity can be sung. A modern reader should keep the absent crew in the moral ledger. A leader’s brilliance under conditions he unnecessarily created is still brilliance—but it is not exoneration.

## Final Reflection

Book 9 suggests that the hardest monster to escape may be the identity that once helped us survive.

Odysseus becomes Nobody and, for a few crucial hours, gains freedom. Without the burden of reputation, he can wait rather than strike, collaborate rather than dominate, hide rather than perform, and let intelligence work without applause. He leads his men from a sealed world into morning.

Then the heroic self demands its payment. The deed must have an author. Polyphemus must know that the apparently insignificant stranger was Odysseus of Ithaca. In naming himself, Odysseus makes his enemy’s recognition more urgent than his companions’ fear and his own return. The name that gathers father, homeland, history, and fame becomes the instrument that endangers them all.

Yet Homer does not ask us to become nobody forever. A name creates relationship with Alcinous and gives shape to the love of home. The lesson is not self-erasure but freedom from compulsory self-display: knowing when identity serves connection and when it serves vanity.

Book 9 therefore leaves us with a severe question. Can we possess our gifts without needing every victory to prove who we are? Intelligence saved Odysseus from the Cyclops. Wisdom would have known when the story was complete—and allowed Nobody to sail away.
