# Book 20 – The Heart That Had to Wait

## What Happened?

Odysseus lies awake on animal skins in the palace. Hearing women leave to join the suitors, he feels murderous anger but orders his heart to endure, recalling how patience helped him escape the Cyclops. Athena appears. He asks how he can defeat so many men and, more troublingly, escape retaliation from their families afterwards. She reassures him and gives him sleep.

Penelope wakes in grief and prays to Artemis for death rather than an unwanted marriage. A dream of Odysseus beside her has made waking harder. At dawn he hears her crying and asks Zeus for signs. Thunder answers from a clear sky. An exhausted woman still grinding grain prays that this will be the suitors' last feast; Odysseus takes her words as confirmation.

The household prepares for a festival of Apollo. Eurycleia corrects Telemachus when he assumes Penelope neglected the guest. Eumaeus brings pigs, Melanthius brings goats and more insults, and the cattle herder Philoetius welcomes the stranger warmly. Philoetius laments having to deliver the livestock he has tended for the suitors to consume. Odysseus promises that his master will soon return, and the herder declares his willingness to help.

The suitors abandon another murder plan after an unfavourable bird omen and resume feasting. Telemachus gives Odysseus an equal portion and demands that the men respect him. Ctesippus mockingly throws a cow's foot as a guest-gift; Odysseus dodges it. Telemachus threatens force, while Agelaus urges him to arrange Penelope's remarriage. He refuses to drive her out against her will.

Athena sends the suitors into unnatural laughter. Their food becomes bloody and their eyes fill with tears. Theoclymenus sees darkness, blood, and departing ghosts. Mocked as mad, he leaves. Telemachus watches his father; Penelope sits within hearing. The narrator closes with a warning of the terrible meal being prepared for the suitors.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 20 keeps us waiting because waiting itself has become the action. Odysseus must endure a night, the household must prepare another feast, and everyone must continue behaving inside arrangements that are close to breaking.

The delay changes the scale of the crisis. From the king's place on the floor, it looks like an intolerable affront. From Penelope's bed, it is the exhaustion of a future she cannot bear. From the mill room, it means more grain to grind after stronger workers have finished. From Philoetius' position, it is years of competent husbandry delivered daily to men who waste it.

These interests overlap without being identical. The worker wants the feasting to stop; Odysseus wants his house restored. The poem briefly lets her voice serve both its own need and his interpretation of destiny. We should hear both.

Odysseus also anticipates the suitors' avengers. Killing men does not remove their relationships from the world. Athena supplies confidence without explaining how the human aftermath will be managed.

Finally, Book 18's laughter becomes horror. Men who misread other people can no longer reliably read their own bodies. Waiting has exposed the lives already caught inside the crisis.

## Major Themes

### 1. Self-Command Requires a Conversation With the Self

Odysseus addresses his heart as though it were another being: remember the Cyclops; you endured worse. He does not deny the anger. He places it within a longer history and interrupts its demand for immediate action.

Book 17 showed restraint from outside, through a body that absorbed a blow. Here we hear its internal labour. The composed person is arguing with impulses we cannot see.

Can memory make us wiser without making us harder? Odysseus uses survival as evidence of capacity, but his delayed intention is still violent. The modern value lies in making room to choose, while also examining what the chosen response is meant to accomplish.

### 2. The Same House Contains Different Emergencies

Odysseus fears a failed attack; Penelope fears the life forced on her if no rescue comes. He receives Athena's direct assurance. She prays into uncertainty. Neither person's suffering can be measured simply by the reader's knowledge that they are close together.

Book 19 made secrecy painful in conversation. The separate sleeping places now make its cost architectural. One roof shelters radically unequal access to hope.

How often do we assume another person's anxiety should diminish because we know something reassuring? Until knowledge is shared, it cannot comfort. Physical proximity does not automatically create a common emotional world.

### 3. Abundance Has Workers Hidden Inside It

The feasting requires grinding, water carrying, cleaning, animal tending, slaughter, and serving. The weakest mill worker is still labouring after her companions have stopped. Philoetius has increased the herds only to watch others consume them.

Book 18 made work an accusation against a beggar. Here we see the labour supporting his accusers. Human bodies pay for the banquet.

Whose time does an apparently effortless pleasure contain? The question reaches any household or institution where comfort at the centre depends on work its beneficiaries rarely witness.

### 4. Revenge Creates an Aftermath

Odysseus asks Athena where he can flee if he succeeds. That conditional question matters: success itself produces danger, because the dead will have avengers.

Book 16's arithmetic of battle becomes a question about living together afterwards.

What would count as completion? Recovering a position and creating peace are different outcomes. Modern conflicts also go wrong when victory is imagined as the disappearance of consequences. Odysseus sees the problem before he has an answer.

### 5. Refusing Reality Can Become a Collective Habit

The suitors dismiss warnings, mock strangers, and turn unease into laughter. Yet they do respond to an unfavourable eagle omen by postponing Telemachus' murder. Their failure is selective: they can heed a sign that changes today's tactics without reconsidering their way of life.

Amphinomus' hesitation in Book 18 becomes the group's habit of adapting around danger.

When does resilience become refusal to learn? A group may absorb every warning as an inconvenience to manage. Theoclymenus finally breaks that pattern by leaving. He lets his judgement change where his body is.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Skins Beneath Odysseus

He lies on the remains of animals the suitors have eaten. Their consumption literally furnishes his bed.

The emptied stores of earlier Books become tangible: even at rest, the owner touches evidence of depletion.

### The Mother Dog and the Turning Sausage

His heart growls like a dog protecting puppies. The warrior's anger receives a maternal image: aggression can arise from guarding what one loves, though that does not settle whether its targets deserve attack.

Then he turns restlessly like a stuffed stomach roasting over a fire. After Book 18's stomach-prize, the man who mastered hunger becomes imaginatively the food being cooked. The two comparisons give heroic endurance a fiercely bodily, almost comic texture. Thought does not free him from the heat.

### Sleep and the Unreachable Bed

Athena gives Odysseus rest; Penelope's dream offers a husband whom waking removes. In Book 19 conversation deferred sleep. Here sleep itself can either restore a person or sharpen loss.

The contrast stops us treating insomnia as a simple failure of will. The poem represents rest as a vulnerable good, sometimes requiring assistance even for its most resourceful character.

### Thunder and the Millstone

Zeus' thunder answers Odysseus' request from outside; the worker's prayer answers from within. Cosmic power and repetitive labour become audible together.

Earlier bird omens prompted royal interpretations. This sign comes from an unnamed worker. Odysseus hears his victory; she asks for relief. Meaning depends on who is listening.

### The Eagle With a Dove

The suitors abandon their murder plan when an eagle appears on the left carrying a dove. Predatory birds have already figured in Telemachus' journey and Penelope's dream.

The would-be attackers briefly accept a limit. An omen can restrain conduct while leaving conscience untouched.

### The Cow's Foot as a Gift

Ctesippus disguises an assault as generosity. He knows the language of hospitality well enough to parody it.

Earlier stools became weapons; now food does. Hospitality's vocabulary survives while its purpose is deliberately reversed.

### Laughter, Blood, and Darkness

Athena makes the suitors laugh beyond ordinary control. Tears and bloody food accompany their merriment, while Theoclymenus describes a hall filling with signs of death.

The household interior, illuminated by Athena in Book 19, becomes dark in the seer's perception. Divine presence can expose or obscure; it does not simply make a room safe. The sequence turns the feast's concealed violence into sensory experience without requiring us to treat the vision as ordinary physical reportage.

## Character Insights

### Odysseus

Odysseus' anxious wakefulness corrects the confidence he performs for others. Even a divine ally does not immediately end his uncertainty.

His restraint postpones killing the women without revising his judgement. Endurance is not necessarily mercy.

The worker's prayer draws his attention to a favourable sign. His purpose shapes what he hears, as purposes often shape ours.

### Penelope

Penelope's wish for death expresses the severity of a life reduced to unwanted alternatives. Her story of Pandareus' daughters imagines girls carefully prepared for marriage only to be carried away into service to the Furies.

The myth makes the destruction of a hoped-for future speakable. It continues Book 19's nightingale comparison, giving grief a story larger than a private complaint. [Gregory Nagy's commentary traces how this myth shapes her prayer to Artemis](https://classical-inquiries.chs.harvard.edu/a-sampling-of-comments-on-odyssey-rhapsody-20/).

By the end she is listening to the hall. Despair has not erased her attention; attentiveness does not mean despair has passed.

### The Mill Worker and the Household Women

The worker has no name, yet a recognisable situation: the household's appetite outlasts her strength. She finishes last because she is weaker, not because she cares less. Exhaustion reveals a limit that the people eating have no reason to notice.

The other women appear laughing, carrying water, cleaning, and serving. These are different actions by a differentiated population. The poem's condemnatory view of some women's sexual relationships must not turn every worker into the same moral category.

### Philoetius

Philoetius recognises possible greatness in the beggar but does not need proof before taking his hand. His welcome turns the principle of hospitality into contact.

His loyalty is argued rather than automatic. He has considered leaving, feels bound to Telemachus, resents his situation, and hopes for Odysseus. He also knows the value of his own work: the flourishing herd testifies to his competence.

His attachment includes years of work and obligation: abundance is something he has tended.

### Telemachus and Eurycleia

Telemachus protects the guest more decisively, gives him an equal portion, and refuses to expel Penelope against her will. Yet he begins by unfairly criticising her hospitality. Eurycleia corrects him with facts.

Boldness toward the suitors coexists with carelessness toward his mother. Eurycleia checks his emerging authority: confidence does not entitle him to invent blame.

She then organises the labour required for the feast. Her competence sustains the very gathering the household longs to end.

### Eumaeus and Melanthius

Both deliver valuable animals and help serve the suitors. Their outward participation looks similar; their allegiances differ.

Eumaeus asks after the stranger; Melanthius abuses him. Shared work does not disclose whether someone endorses, resents, or merely survives the arrangement requiring it.

### Ctesippus, Agelaus, and the Leading Suitors

Ctesippus contributes a fresh variation on cruelty: a supposed gift that is really a missile. Wealth gives him confidence that amusement will excuse injury.

Agelaus politely offers a marriage settlement as relief from conditions his own group maintains. Reasonable language can carry pressure.

Antinous acknowledges that divine obstruction has prevented Telemachus' murder; Amphinomus redirects the group after the omen; Eurymachus mocks the seer. Their styles differ, yet none leaves the feast.

### Theoclymenus, Athena, and Zeus

Theoclymenus answers mockery by naming his intact senses and departing for Piraeus' welcome. Unlike the uneasy Amphinomus, he turns perception into an exit.

Zeus supplies thunder; Athena offers rest and intensifies disorder. The gods participate in the crisis through both relief and escalation.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Homicide Implicated Families

Odysseus' fear of avengers reflects a world in which kinship carried obligations of retaliation and redress. A killing could create danger beyond the immediate encounter, even for a powerful man who considered himself justified.

This helps explain his anxiety without importing a single later Greek legal code into the epic. The text itself gives the essential fact: victory over the suitors would not automatically end the conflict.

### Festival and Feast Joined Worship to Labour

The day belongs to Apollo. Sacrifice, communal assembly, and eating are linked, while the household's workers prepare the setting and food.

Religious observance does not prevent the suitors from plotting murder or insulting a guest. The disjunction is deliberate: performing sacred forms does not guarantee conduct worthy of them.

### Wilson's Attention to Enslaved Lives Changes the View

Wilson's [discussion of slavery and sexuality in the poem](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1q3z25w) emphasises that elite domestic life depends on people whose labour and bodies are subject to ownership. That perspective is consequential here: Butler's language of servants and sexual misconduct can make hierarchy feel like ordinary household management.

Keeping enslavement explicit does not establish what every woman wanted. It requires us to distinguish personal desire, physical force, and structural dependence. Telemachus' complaint about women being dragged through the house supplies evidence of coercion alongside the opening description of some women going willingly to the suitors. [Linguistic scholarship discusses precisely this distinction](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/Dike/article/download/12251/11543/36670). Neither detail should erase the other.

### Signs Could Include Unintended Speech

Odysseus asks for a spoken sign before the mill worker prays. She addresses Zeus about her own exhaustion, not the disguised man about his plan. The divine message, as he receives it, need not be intentional on the speaker's part.

Thunder supports his interpretation. The ethical question remains: does hearing a sign also allow him to hear the speaker?

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Address the Impulse Before It Chooses the Action

Odysseus speaks to his heart and remembers a previous ordeal. Creating that small distance can help us respond deliberately. It does not settle what response is right; it gives judgement time to operate.

### Plan for the Morning After Success

His question about avengers belongs inside the plan, not after it. Before a confrontation, ask which relationships and obligations will still exist if you get what you want.

### Listen for the Work Beneath Abundance

The mill worker's complaint reveals a cost invisible at the table. When judging whether an arrangement works well, include the experience of the people who must keep it running.

### Let Correction Improve Authority

Eurycleia gives Telemachus facts that contradict his accusation. Leadership requires room for that reply. Being newly responsible makes accurate correction more valuable, not less.

### Distinguish a Tactical Retreat From a Moral Change

The suitors postpone murder but continue their occupation. An avoided act is good, yet it need not mean the underlying intention has changed. Watch what the next decision preserves.

## Stoic Dialogue

### The Heart and the Pause Before Assent

At the beginning of the Book, Odysseus prevents anger from becoming immediate violence. [Epictetus' *Enchiridion*, section 20](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html), likewise recommends gaining time before being carried away by an insult and one's judgement of it.

The agreement concerns the pause. The difference concerns its aim: Odysseus postpones retaliation so he can undertake a larger one. Stoic discipline would also examine whether revenge mistakes injury to status for injury to moral character. The useful question today is not simply whether we stayed calm, but whether reflection changed what we considered justified.

### Athena's Promise and the Demand for Certainty

Odysseus wants assurance both of victory and escape. Athena can provide divine confidence; an ordinary person cannot obtain that kind of guarantee.

In [*Discourses* 2.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/discourses.2.two.html), Epictetus distinguishes circumstances beyond choice from the care with which one uses them. The agreement is practical preparation amid uncertainty. The difference is where security lies: divine rescue of an external project versus fidelity to good judgement whatever happens to it.

That distinction need not belittle Odysseus' danger. It asks whether responsible action is possible before success is assured.

### The Mill Worker's Pain

A Stoic may maintain that bodily hardship cannot by itself make someone morally bad. The worker's prayer reminds us why that must never become permission to ignore hardship. Her pain is not a philosophical mistake.

The comparison sets a limit on easy lessons about endurance. We can admire what someone bears while recognising our obligation to make the burden lighter. The banquet does not become just because its workers are resilient.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. What do you say to yourself when an impulse demands immediate action—and does it help you choose wisely?
2. Have you ever obtained a victory without having imagined what it would require afterwards?
3. Whose exhaustion is absent from your picture of a comfortable home or successful organisation?
4. What does Penelope's suffering reveal about the limits of reassurance that remains unspoken?
5. How can gratitude for loyalty coexist with respect for someone's reasons to leave?
6. When have you heard another person's complaint mainly as confirmation of your own plans?
7. Does changing tactics in response to danger ever allow you to avoid changing your mind?
8. What enables Theoclymenus to leave a room in which everyone else keeps laughing?

## Connections

### Book 9: The Cave Inside the House

Odysseus explicitly recalls the Cyclops. Home now requires a survival skill learned in a monstrous dwelling: defer the satisfying act until escape or success becomes possible.

The palace should let survival tactics cease. Instead, he experiences home through a memory of entrapment.

### Plato's Divided Soul

[In *Republic* 4, Plato quotes Odysseus rebuking his heart](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.5.iv.html) to support a distinction between reasoning and anger. Here the connection is direct literary reception: a Homeric moment becomes evidence in a later account of the mind.

Homer gives us a man who argues with himself; Plato asks what that argument reveals about the structure of the self. The later theory does not exhaust the scene's bodily restlessness or its moral ambiguity.

### Book 18: Hearing a Warning and Leaving

Amphinomus heard the disguised king's warning and returned to his seat. Theoclymenus announces his perception and goes to Piraeus.

Their positions and risks differ. Still, they give us two responses to unwelcome insight: troubled membership or the social cost of departure.

### Shakespeare's Haunted Banquet

The banquet in *Macbeth* offers a useful comparison: the outward forms of feasting coexist with a vision of violence that some present cannot or will not see. This is an analogy, not evidence of direct borrowing from Book 20.

Both scenes make a gathering unstable by changing what it means to look around the room. A feast becomes frightening when hospitality's familiar forms can no longer contain the harm associated with it.

## Memorable Passage

> “And there is this further difficulty, which is still more considerable. Supposing that with Jove's and your assistance I succeed in killing them, I must ask you to consider where I am to escape to from their avengers when it is all over.”

— Samuel Butler translation, Book 20

The unexpected phrase is “when it is all over.” Odysseus immediately shows why it would not be over. The apparent end contains another beginning, because opponents have kin and violence produces claims beyond its first occasion.

This is intelligence extending beyond the dramatic climax. Homer allows the hero to understand the inadequacy of the very ending he is pursuing. The passage asks us to measure plans by the life they leave possible afterwards.

## Worth Arguing With

Athena does more than support Odysseus. She sustains the suitors' insulting behaviour and unsettles their minds. The narrative also insists that their own wrongdoing brings their doom. How should responsibility be understood when a goddess intensifies the acts that justify punishment?

One reading treats divine action as the epic expression of an already established character: the men become fully what their repeated choices have made them. Another stresses how little freedom remains once divine vengeance takes over. The poem supports discussion of both, but its ending judgement should not stop us asking the question.

There is also a danger in admiring Odysseus' opening restraint too quickly. He considers killing the women and decides to wait. That demonstrates control of timing; it does not establish proportionate judgement or attention to their different circumstances. The Book invites admiration for endurance while supplying reasons to examine the violence being preserved inside it.

## Final Reflection

Book 20 asks us to spend time with people who cannot yet act as they wish. A man argues with his heart. A woman wakes from the comfort of a dream into grief. Another woman keeps grinding because her body cannot meet the pace imposed on it. A herder wonders how long loyalty can require him to deliver the results of his labour into someone else's waste.

Waiting looks different from each place. That is why the Book's delay matters. If we hurry toward the confrontation, we risk understanding the house only as the prize of its returning owner. Homer lets us hear the lives already being spent inside it.

Odysseus' question about avengers carries those lives into the future. He knows that an apparent solution can create another emergency. For the moment, divine reassurance allows him to rest, but the human problem remains before the reader.

Perhaps the Book's most useful lesson is to enlarge what we mean by a successful action. It must be judged against the impulse it restrains, the people who bear its costs, and the world that follows it. Theoclymenus leaves; the suitors keep laughing; Telemachus watches his father. We are waiting too, with a clearer sense of what any ending will have to answer for.
