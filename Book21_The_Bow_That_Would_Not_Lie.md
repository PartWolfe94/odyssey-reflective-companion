# Book 21 – The Bow That Would Not Lie

## What Happened?

Prompted by Athena, Penelope retrieves Odysseus' bow from the storeroom. The narrator explains its history: Iphitus gave it to Odysseus in friendship, before Heracles killed Iphitus in violation of hospitality. Odysseus kept the bow at home as a reminder of his friend. Penelope weeps over it, then brings it to the suitors and announces the contest she described the previous night. She will marry the man who can string it and shoot through twelve axes.

Telemachus sets out the axes and tries the bow himself. After three unsuccessful attempts, he would have succeeded on the fourth, but Odysseus signals him to stop. The suitors then try. Leiodes fails first and speaks ominously about the contest. The others cannot string the bow even after warming and greasing it.

Outside, Odysseus questions Eumaeus and Philoetius about their loyalty, reveals himself, and shows them his scar. They embrace him. He promises homes, wives, and a lasting relationship with Telemachus if they prevail, then assigns their tasks: bring him the bow and secure the doors.

Eurymachus fails and admits that being exposed as inferior to Odysseus troubles him more than losing Penelope. Antinous proposes postponement on account of Apollo's festival. The disguised Odysseus asks to try. The suitors object, but Penelope defends his request and offers gifts if he succeeds. Telemachus claims authority over the bow and sends her upstairs.

Eumaeus carries the bow through threats and jeering to Odysseus. Eurycleia closes the women's quarters; Philoetius secures the courtyard gates. Odysseus inspects the bow, strings it with a musician's ease, and plucks a note like a swallow's call. Zeus thunders. Still seated, he shoots through all twelve axes. At his signal, Telemachus arms himself and stands beside him.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 19 established identity through intimate evidence. Book 21 makes identity visible as an action no invented biography can perform. The beggar handles the bow; the room's social assumptions begin to collapse.

But the contest is not simply a device that recognises its rightful owner. Telemachus could string it too. The difference is that he stops when his father signals. The son proves his growing strength by approaching success, then proves his partnership by relinquishing the moment. The object tests coordination and judgement as well as muscle.

Before Odysseus can touch it, others must make his demonstration possible. Penelope retrieves it and argues that the stranger should be allowed a turn. Eumaeus carries it despite threats. Philoetius secures the gates. The apparently solitary feat depends on a network of decisions.

The bow also arrives with a history that unsettles heroic admiration. Heracles, a famous performer of mighty deeds, murdered the friend who gave it. The narrator condemns him. Physical greatness has already been separated from good conduct before the strongest archer takes his seat.

Ridicule finally gives way to a tuned string. Odysseus produces a note, then a shot. Demonstrated ability brings satisfaction and a further question: what will the person who commands this room do with that command?

## Major Themes

### 1. A Test Reveals What People Are Really Protecting

Eurymachus admits that losing the marriage matters less than being remembered as weaker than Odysseus. Later he explains why the beggar must not try: ordinary people might talk about his success.

The appetite exposed in Books 17–18 has become anxiety about reputation. The suitors' stated goal is marriage; their immediate concern is preserving superiority in other people's eyes. Failure hurts most where it contradicts the story they tell about themselves.

What do we defend when a test feels intolerable? We may claim to protect standards while protecting our standing. To the suitors, the beggar's competence is more frightening than his need.

### 2. Inheritance Must Become Ability

Telemachus arranges the axes competently and nearly strings the bow. He no longer needs only to assert that this is his father's house; he begins to exhibit the capacity associated with that inheritance.

Book 16 made him a strategic partner. Here his capability approaches his father's in a visible trial. Yet succession does not require immediate replacement: he can contribute without insisting on occupying the decisive position.

When does maturity mean taking the lead, and when does it mean yielding it? The signal makes his apparent failure a successful act of cooperation. A team can lose something important when every member must publicly prove everything they can do.

### 3. Trust Changes When It Gives Someone a Future

In Book 14 Eumaeus sheltered a stranger; in Book 20 Philoetius welcomed him. Now Odysseus reveals himself and promises both men a more secure place within the household's future.

The embrace expresses affection; the promised homes give it material form. Loyalty becomes a basis for shared action, rather than only evidence Odysseus collects.

Is loyalty fully acknowledged if its beneficiaries offer only gratitude? The scene invites that question without creating modern equality. Odysseus still controls the gifts, marriages, and terms of belonging. Reciprocity improves their position within an unequal order.

### 4. Skill Begins With Attention

While the suitors worry about what spectators will say, Odysseus examines whether the bow has deteriorated. He attends to its actual condition before testing it.

This continues the practical competence he claimed in Book 18: mowing, ploughing, fighting. Knowledge lives in an understanding of materials and the ability to work with them. His expertise looks quiet because the dramatic effort has been absorbed into experience.

What changes when we approach a task as something to understand rather than an occasion to defend ourselves? His ease shows what long familiarity can look like from outside.

### 5. A Necessary Participant Can Still Be Excluded

Penelope supplies the bow, states the terms, and answers the suitors' objections. Then Telemachus tells her to return to the loom while he handles the weapon. She does not witness the successful shot.

His words echo the silencing in Book 1, but the circumstances have changed. He now knows a plan she does not, and her removal also keeps her away from danger. Practical protection and assertion of male authority occupy the same speech.

Does usefulness in creating a decision guarantee a voice in its outcome? Her departure warns against reading increased confidence in the son as uncomplicated progress for everyone in the household.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Key, the Threshold, and the Stored Bow

Penelope possesses the key and opens the room containing her husband's treasures. The bow has remained among preserved clothing and fragrant stores throughout his absence.

Doors have repeatedly separated belonging from exclusion. Here a woman opens the door through which the decisive object enters the action. The description of careful carpentry also directs attention toward the accumulated work that makes a household durable.

The bow is not a trophy carried back from Troy. It belongs to Odysseus' life before the war, like the childhood scar in Book 19. Return reaches further back than military fame.

### Iphitus' Gift

The bow preserves a friendship cut short before the two men could visit each other's homes. Its personal value therefore exceeds its usefulness as a weapon.

Heracles' killing of his guest places broken hospitality inside the object's history. After many bad hosts and violated gifts, the contest begins with a reminder that extraordinary courage does not exempt anyone from ordinary obligations.

### The Scar Shown Voluntarily

Eurycleia discovered the scar by touch in Book 19, disrupting concealment. Here Odysseus deliberately displays it to secure trust.

The same mark serves different purposes according to who controls its disclosure. Bodily history becomes the bridge from a conditional promise of loyalty to recognition and an embrace.

### The Axes and the Fourth Attempt

Telemachus sets the axes in a straight trench and packs the earth around them. Order must be built before skill can be measured.

His interrupted fourth attempt prevents the trial from becoming a simple contrast between a uniquely capable father and everyone else. The narrative explicitly grants the son unrealised success. We see something the suitors do not: a capable person choosing to look incapable.

The precise arrangement through which the arrow passes remains disputed. Butler supplies handle-holes; [scholarly reconstructions offer competing accounts](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/greece-and-rome/article/abs/odysseus-and-the-axes-homeric-ballistics-reconstructed/7C090A218588C05A001FF4B0A49CF94F). The literary point does not require us to pretend the mechanics are settled.

### The Ship's Cable

Philoetius secures the outer gates with a cable lying in the gatehouse. Equipment associated with travel now prevents departure.

The sea journey's materials acquire a domestic purpose. Alongside the removal of weapons in Book 19, the fastening turns the house into a controlled space. Hospitality's open entrance becomes a strategic boundary.

### Lyre, Swallow, and Thunder

Odysseus strings the bow like a singer fitting a lyre string. Its note resembles a swallow; Zeus answers with thunder. Human craft, animal sound, and divine confirmation converge.

Book 8 made song disclose the hero through his tears. Here the hero produces something like music himself. The comparison joins bodily skill to the artistry that has shaped his stories.

[Gregory Nagy connects the swallow and the scene's festival language with seasonal renewal and Apollo](https://classical-inquiries.chs.harvard.edu/a-sampling-of-comments-on-odyssey-rhapsody-21/). That reading adds a public rhythm of return to this intensely private recovery, without making the weapon harmless.

## Character Insights

### Odysseus

Odysseus asks to test whether suffering has diminished his strength. The request fits his disguise but also poses a real question about return: can an old capacity survive long disuse and hardship?

His inspection is careful rather than anxious. He knows the instrument well enough to distinguish material problems from performance pressure.

Outside, his kisses on the herdsmen's hands and faces make recognition unusually reciprocal. He needs their agency now, not merely their testimony. Yet his claim that only these two have prayed for him reflects what he has observed, not exhaustive knowledge of every person's loyalty.

### Penelope

Penelope grieves over the bow before making it available for a contest that may separate her from the house. Public decisiveness rests on private sorrow.

Her most direct judgement concerns reputation: men consuming another person's estate cannot reasonably expect admiration. She refuses the suitors' assumption that humiliation lies in losing to a beggar rather than in what they have already done.

[Wilson cautions against mistaking Penelope's intelligence for equality of power within her marriage and household](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/a-translators-reckoning-with-the-women-of-the-odyssey). Her departure here gives that distinction concrete force: she enables the test but cannot insist on remaining to see it.

### Telemachus

Telemachus' spoken disappointment after his father's signal is a performance. Unlike the suitors, he can bear to appear weaker than he is because he knows what the moment requires.

His treatment of Eumaeus is less comfortable. He calls him father while threatening to stone him if he does not bring the bow. The threat helps move the plan forward and reassures the laughing suitors, but still uses superiority over an enslaved man as its instrument.

He is becoming effective. Whether effectiveness is always admirable remains a separate question.

### Eumaeus and Philoetius

Both men weep over the bow before they know its owner stands beside them. Their grief precedes any prospect of reward.

After recognition, loyalty becomes work: carry, communicate, close, secure. Eumaeus briefly stops under the suitors' threats. His fear does not invalidate his devotion; it shows what devotion must overcome in a room where powerful men can promise him a terrible death.

Philoetius' quiet fastening of the gate is as necessary as the archer's spectacular success. The poem distributes responsibility more widely than its central image suggests.

### Eurymachus and Antinous

Eurymachus is candid about shame. He fears future gossip more than the loss of the woman he claims to desire. His problem is not that he misunderstands the test, but that he understands its social consequences very well.

Antinous manages that anxiety by proposing delay and invoking Apollo's festival. The religious occasion is real; its usefulness as an excuse is striking. His warning about the drunken Centaur likewise uses an inherited moral story to police a guest while ignoring his own conduct.

### Leiodes, Melanthius, and Eurycleia

The narrator distinguishes Leiodes from the others by saying he disapproves of their wrongdoing. Yet he remains their ritual specialist and a participant in the contest. Disapproval has not made him leave.

Melanthius supplies fire and fat; Eurycleia closes the women's doors. Practical household knowledge can serve different allegiances. Obedient actions alone do not reveal whose future a person is helping to create.

### Iphitus, Heracles, and Athena

Iphitus initiates friendship through exchange; Heracles destroys him while hosting him. The memory provides a sharp internal criticism of heroic power.

Athena prompts Penelope's action and later gives her sleep. The goddess's orchestration makes several human intentions converge without granting each participant knowledge of the whole design.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Gifts Carried Relationships

The bow records an exchange of weapons between men establishing friendship. Such gifts could support continuing obligations across households. Here death prevents that relationship from fully developing, so the object preserves an interrupted possibility.

Its story recalls the gifts of Telemachus' journey while making reciprocity personal: value includes who gave an object and what future it once promised.

### Marriage and Competition Meet Uneasily

Penelope uses a contest to select a husband, drawing marriage into the world of public excellence and prizes. But when the stranger asks to try, she expressly denies that his success would make him her husband. She offers clothing, weapons, and safe passage instead.

The rules therefore do not operate like an impartial modern selection procedure. Ability can cross a social boundary more readily than marriage can.

### Apollo Makes the Excuse Ironic

The festival belongs to the god associated with archery and music. Antinous uses it to postpone archery; Odysseus' bow produces music-like sound on that same occasion.

Religious timing can mean different things to different participants. The day's holiness is no guarantee that those invoking it understand its significance.

### Reward Is Not Yet Equality

Odysseus' promises give Eumaeus and Philoetius a substantial future: households, land, wives, and closeness to Telemachus. The precise legal implications are not spelled out here.

We can recognise an expansion of belonging without converting it into a modern declaration of equal rights. The ruler remains the person who can grant these benefits.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Notice Which Tests You Want to Prevent

The suitors fear allowing an unexpected person to succeed. When we object to someone's opportunity, it is worth asking whether the objection protects fairness or protects us from comparison.

### Give Attention Before Giving Force

Odysseus inspects the bow. Sometimes expertise begins with checking the condition of the task rather than making a greater effort against an imagined problem.

### Let Another Person's Moment Remain Theirs

Telemachus can stop short of proving himself. Cooperation sometimes means knowing that a contribution matters even when its full extent stays invisible.

### Make Gratitude Material

The promised homes acknowledge that loyalty requires a future. Appreciation becomes more credible when it changes the conditions of the person being thanked.

### Count the People Behind a Solitary Achievement

The final shot needs Penelope's key, Eumaeus' delivery, Philoetius' cable, and Telemachus' restraint. An impressive performance can conceal the cooperation that allowed it to happen.

## Stoic Dialogue

### Eurymachus and Borrowed Excellence

In [*Enchiridion* 6](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html), Epictetus warns against pride in excellences that belong to possessions rather than one's own judgement. The suitors inhabit an impressive estate and handle a famous bow, but cannot turn proximity to greatness into ability.

Homer and the Stoic meet in exposing that confusion. They separate at the next step: archery proves something important about Odysseus, while a Stoic would insist that physical skill alone does not establish virtue. The modern question is which achievements genuinely belong to our conduct, rather than our setting or associations.

### The Herdsmen and the Limits of Rank

Odysseus' embrace and promised friendship invite comparison with [Seneca's Letter 47](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_47), which asks masters to recognise humanity and character beneath enslaved status.

Both offer recognition across hierarchy; neither text supplies a modern abolitionist programme. Seneca's language of friendship still operates within mastery. That limit helps us appreciate the embrace without imagining the social distance has vanished. Courtesy and gratitude matter, but should prompt us to examine the power that still governs the relationship.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. Which matters more to the suitors: failing the task or being seen failing it?
2. When have you chosen not to display an ability because cooperation required something else?
3. What does an object's history contribute to its value beyond usefulness or price?
4. Can a protective act also silence the person it protects?
5. What makes gratitude sufficient when another person's loyalty has materially sustained you?
6. Who makes your most apparently independent achievements possible?
7. Why does Homer place Heracles' violation of hospitality inside the history of this triumphant weapon?

## Connections

### Books 8 and 19: From Listening to Making

In Book 8 a singer drew Odysseus' hidden identity toward disclosure; in Book 19 Odysseus crafted plausible stories. Now his hands are compared to a musician's. The poem joins the hero's control of instruments to his control of narrative.

The comparison also includes the poet telling this scene: careful preparation makes an ending appear effortless.

### Arthur and the Test of the Weapon

The sword-in-the-stone tradition offers a useful comparison with a weapon that reveals authority others cannot claim. The resemblance does not establish direct borrowing.

Homer's scene is more complicated than a test that selects one predestined person. Telemachus could succeed, and access to the bow is contested. Recognition depends on social decisions as well as exceptional ability.

### The Centaur Story as Misused Moral Instruction

Antinous tells of Eurytion's drunken outrage and punishment among the Lapiths. The story condemns a guest's destructive excess, yet its teller has spent years violating another household's hospitality.

This is a recurring danger in the use of myths, histories, and workplace anecdotes: a familiar lesson can become a weapon against someone else while exempting the speaker from its force.

## Memorable Passage

> “Then he took it in his right hand to prove the string, and it sang sweetly under his touch like the twittering of a swallow.”

— Samuel Butler translation, Book 21

The quietness is what makes the sound decisive. After the suitors' exertions and anxious talk, a small, controlled action changes the room.

The swallow gives a deadly instrument a living voice. Familiarity, return, and danger occupy the same note. We hear recovered skill before its consequences, and the beauty of the sound briefly holds those possibilities together. Homer allows us to enjoy mastery while making its purpose impossible to forget.

## Worth Arguing With

The contest makes the suitors' pretensions visible, but does it prove a right to rule or marry? Strength and technique can establish competence at archery. They cannot by themselves establish justice, good government, or a woman's consent.

The poem itself complicates an easy equation. Heracles was mighty and murdered a guest. Telemachus could string the bow but yields his turn. Penelope says success would not entitle the beggar to marry her. These details prevent the trial from being a simple moral sorting machine, even while the story makes Odysseus' success thrilling.

We should also examine the son's dismissal of his mother. The plan supplies a practical reason for removing her, but Telemachus speaks in the language of men's exclusive authority. She is asked to accept the consequences of an action whose crucial information has been withheld from her. Admiring the strategy need not end our interest in that unequal arrangement.

## Final Reflection

Book 21 ends with a son standing armed beside his seated father. The image gathers many kinds of achievement: strength recovered, patience maintained, loyalty disclosed, a shared signal understood. The bow has answered a question that insults and boasts could not settle.

Yet the scene's wisdom is larger than the satisfaction of watching the underestimated man succeed. Odysseus reaches the weapon because other people open doors, carry objects, resist intimidation, and trust what they have learned. His mastery is personal; the conditions that make it possible are shared.

Telemachus contributes something particularly difficult to see. He accepts an appearance of failure when he might have achieved public success. Penelope contributes the object and the argument that puts it within reach, then leaves before the result. The triumph contains work and restraint the celebrating observer could easily miss.

Perhaps this Book asks us to look more carefully at excellence: to recognise the attention within apparently effortless skill, the support within apparent independence, and the difference between proving ability and proving moral worth. The bow's history has already shown that a great hero can behave shamefully.

For a moment, the string sings. We can admire the note while remaining responsible for asking what follows from the power it announces.
