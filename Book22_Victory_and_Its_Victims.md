# Book 22 – Victory and Its Victims

## What Happened?

Odysseus casts off his rags, takes position with his arrows, and shoots Antinous as he raises a drinking cup. The suitors initially assume the shot was accidental. Odysseus then declares who he is and accuses them of consuming his estate, violating the women, and courting his wife. Eurymachus blames Antinous and offers compensation. Odysseus refuses, and the men fight for their lives.

Eurymachus attacks and is killed by Odysseus; Telemachus kills Amphinomus. The son fetches armour for their four-man force but leaves the storeroom open. Melanthius supplies weapons to the suitors. Telemachus admits his mistake, and Eumaeus and Philoetius capture Melanthius on his second trip, binding and suspending him in pain.

Athena appears as Mentor, challenges Odysseus to show his old courage, and helps the defenders. Eventually she displays the aegis, breaking the suitors' resistance. Odysseus and his companions kill the fleeing men. Leiodes begs for mercy, claiming that he opposed their wrongdoing; Odysseus rejects his plea and kills him. The singer Phemius appeals for his life on the grounds that he was compelled to perform. Telemachus confirms his innocence and also saves the herald Medon.

Eurycleia enters the blood-filled hall. Odysseus stops her cry of triumph and asks which women have been disloyal. She identifies twelve. They are forced to remove the bodies and clean the room. Odysseus orders them killed with swords, but Telemachus chooses hanging as a more dishonourable death. Melanthius is then mutilated and killed.

Odysseus purifies the house with fire and sulphur. The surviving household women gather around him with torches, embracing and kissing him. Remembering them, he is moved toward tears. Penelope has not yet rejoined him.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 22 fulfils a long-promised revenge and makes fulfilment more disturbing than anticipation. The men who abused a beggar now discover that they cannot escape him. But the narrative does not end when the armed resistance ends. It continues through pleas, selective mercy, torture, forced cleaning, execution, purification, and affection.

That extended ending changes the reader's task. During the battle we can follow strategy: arrows, doorways, armour, numbers. Afterwards, tactical necessity no longer explains everything. We must ask how people are judged and what distinguishes protection from punishment, punishment from degradation.

Telemachus' intervention for Phemius and Medon proves that Odysseus can change his judgement. That possibility makes the rejected plea of Leiodes and the unheard cases of the women harder to pass over.

The final embrace refuses an equally easy conclusion in the opposite direction. Odysseus is not revealed as incapable of affection. He remembers the surviving women and nearly weeps. The same household contains relief and terror; the same man can restore belonging for some and destroy others.

Homer has made us want the suitors stopped. This Book asks what we do with the desire once it is satisfied. We may recognise the justice of ending an occupation without assuming that every act committed in its ending is therefore just.

## Major Themes

### 1. Being Right About the Wrong Does Not Settle the Remedy

The suitors' offences are real: exploitation, violence, threats to Telemachus, and the corruption of hospitality. Eurymachus acknowledges wrongdoing but makes Antinous bear the blame. His proposed repayment could be expedient bargaining rather than repentance.

Yet rejecting that proposal still leaves a choice about response. Book 20 asked what would follow victory. Book 22 shows how quickly a demand for justice can become a determination that no payment and no surrender will suffice.

What makes a remedy proportionate? We need not trust Eurymachus to recognise the question. A valid grievance does not justify every response.

### 2. Judgement Depends on Who Gets Heard

Leiodes, Phemius, and Medon seek survival in the same hall. Phemius explains compulsion; Telemachus confirms it and recalls Medon's kindness. Leiodes receives a different response: Odysseus infers hostile prayers from his role among the suitors and kills him while he is still speaking.

Book 17 distinguished degrees of conduct within the group. Here distinctions affect survival unevenly. Knowing the right witness can matter as much as making a truthful claim.

How fair is a decision if only some have an advocate? Wherever authority distinguishes participation from willing collaboration, an opportunity to explain is itself a form of power.

### 3. Maturity Can Enlarge Mercy and Cruelty Together

Telemachus admits leaving the storeroom open and saves two noncombatants. Both actions show independent judgement: he can acknowledge fault and interrupt his father's momentum.

Then he changes the women's sentence from killing by swords to hanging, expressly seeking a death that denies honour. The self-assertion developed since Book 1 has acquired lethal reach.

What does growing into authority mean? Courage and decisiveness are capacities, not guarantees about their use. Telemachus becomes more responsible in one decision and more brutal in the next.

### 4. Dehumanising Images Do Not Have One Effect

The suitors become fleeing cattle, small birds under attack, and fish dying on shore. Odysseus resembles a blood-covered lion. Such images magnify the victors' force while also making the defeated look helpless.

Predatory birds previously promised return in omens and dreams. Now their violence is realised in the hall. The women, in turn, are compared to birds caught near their nests. The language of homecoming extends to people being denied any homecoming of their own.

Are we invited to cheer, pity, recoil, or all three? A simile can support the action's momentum while making us feel what its moral categories suppress. Reading carefully means not discarding that unease.

### 5. Restoring Order Does Not Erase Its Cost

The condemned women clean the hall before being killed. Fire and sulphur follow. Finally the surviving women embrace Odysseus. Physical disorder is removed, ritual order restored, and familiar relationships resume.

Book 20 made the labour behind the feast visible. Here the labour behind its destruction becomes visible too. Those about to die must make the house habitable for its survivors.

Can a clean room stand for a reconciled community? Modern institutions also make crises look finished before their human meaning has been confronted. Cleanliness is an achievement; innocence is a different claim.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Falling Cup

Antinous dies while preparing to drink. His cup falls and the food spills as blood enters the feast.

The man who governed access to another person's provisions can no longer complete an ordinary act of consumption. Book 20's bloody banquet vision becomes concrete bodily ruin. The horror lies partly in how abruptly the familiar action is interrupted.

### The Doorway and the Open Storeroom

Odysseus' position at the entrance compensates for his smaller force. Control of space, prepared across Books 19–21, becomes essential to survival.

One unsecured door changes the balance. Telemachus' admission prevents a practical error from hardening into a false accusation against the women. The architecture gives moral meaning to a small operational fact: responsibility begins with saying what actually happened.

### The Swallow and the Aegis

Athena takes the form of a swallow in the rafters after appearing as Mentor. Book 21's bowstring sounded like that bird; now the supporting divine presence occupies the same acoustic and visual world.

Her eventual display of the aegis breaks resistance through terror. The contrast between small bird and overwhelming emblem reminds us that visible size need not measure power. Her intervention also limits any account of victory as a purely human contest.

### The Spear as a Return Gift

Philoetius calls his killing of Ctesippus repayment for the cow's foot thrown in Book 20. Hospitality's language has become a language of retaliation.

The reversal satisfies a desire for repayment. But a degrading mock-gift is answered with death: reciprocity alone cannot establish proportion.

### Predators, Fish, and Trapped Birds

The attacking men resemble birds of prey; the fallen suitors resemble fish deprived of water. These comparisons move from energetic pursuit to creatures whose resistance has ended.

The hanging women resemble thrushes or doves caught while approaching their nests. They are granted no individual speeches, yet the image gives them a direction of desire: shelter. The poem's own language makes it harder to treat them simply as dirt removed from the house.

### The Cable, the Sponge, and the Sulphur

A ship's cable becomes an instrument of hanging. Book 21 used a cable to secure the gates; here maritime equipment acquires a more terrible purpose. Cleaning equipment moves alongside weapons; purification follows killing.

Ordinary domestic and maritime objects have acquired terrible uses. The sequence is a reminder that cruelty need not require an alien setting or unfamiliar tools. It can be organised through the materials of daily life.

### Torches and the Final Embrace

The surviving women arrive carrying lights and touch Odysseus' head, shoulders, and hands. Their embrace recalls earlier recognitions and restores personal memory to a scene dominated by bodies as targets.

That warmth belongs to the survivors. The placement asks us to hold their relief beside the absence of the women who cannot join them. Affection can be real without providing a verdict on everything that preceded it.

## Character Insights

### Odysseus

Odysseus' strategic competence does not make him invulnerable. The armed suitors frighten him, and he needs his son's equipment, the herdsmen's intervention, and Athena's help.

His moral attention is capable of discrimination but strongly shaped by his position. He accepts testimony for Phemius and Medon; he infers what Leiodes must have prayed; he asks Eurycleia to identify the women. In Book 19 he insisted he could judge them himself. Now another person's classification supplies the condemned group.

His prohibition on boasting over the dead is serious. So is the command to torment Melanthius. We should resist resolving that contradiction by treating either restraint or cruelty as unreal.

### Telemachus

The most constructive words Telemachus speaks concern his own mistake. He takes responsibility for the unlocked storeroom at a moment when blame could easily settle on a subordinate.

He also acts as a witness who changes outcomes. His knowledge of Phemius and Medon interrupts indiscriminate danger.

The hanging turns his independence toward degradation. Eurycleia explains that Penelope previously limited his authority over the women; now he determines how they will die. Alleged insults to himself form part of his justification, while the condemned have no opportunity to answer him.

### Eumaeus and Philoetius

Their skill and courage help save Odysseus. Yet participation in the victorious household also draws them into torment and execution. Eumaeus mocks the suspended Melanthius; Philoetius turns a spear into a retaliatory gift.

Their earlier compassion remains real. Care for one's own group can coexist with cruelty toward outsiders; loyalty can organise both.

### Eurycleia and the Women

Eurycleia sees liberation from the suitors and begins to rejoice. Her account distinguishes twelve women from the fifty in the house, but reports disrespect and disloyal conduct rather than individual histories of coercion, choice, or danger.

The condemned women first appear here as a collective in tears. They carry bodies and clean under orders before being killed. Their lack of separate names and defences narrows what the reader can know; it cannot establish that all their cases were identical.

The survivors' welcome adds another response within an enslaved household containing different attachments and experiences.

### Eurymachus, Amphinomus, and the Fighting Suitors

Eurymachus first negotiates, then organises resistance. His transfer of blame to the dead Antinous is self-serving, but his diagnosis of the immediate situation is clear: Odysseus offers no quarter.

Amphinomus, previously uneasy and comparatively courteous, attacks and is killed. The narrator also grants courage to the men still fighting. Courage does not absolve their conduct, but neither does wrongdoing make every human quality disappear.

### Leiodes, Phemius, and Medon

Leiodes' claim that he opposed the suitors has support in Book 21's narration. Odysseus instead judges his ritual role as evidence of hostile desire. The gap between what the reader knows and what the victor accepts is disturbing.

Phemius combines a plea of compulsion with an appeal to the value of his singing. Medon emerges from hiding when he hears Telemachus name him. Their survival depends on character, circumstance, usefulness, and a credible advocate—not on a single transparent test.

### Melanthius and Athena

Melanthius actively arms the opposition and endangers the four defenders. That establishes his role in combat; it does not make his prolonged torment tactically necessary after capture.

Athena tests father and son before breaking their opponents. Her support does not resolve the human choices about captivity and punishment.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Supplication Was a Claim, Not an Automatic Guarantee

Clasping a person's knees formally appeals for protection. Both Leiodes and Phemius use the gesture, but it does not produce the same response.

The ritual places vulnerability before the stronger person without compelling mercy. Authority must interpret the claim; a witness can reinforce it.

### Compensation Had a Place in Disputes

Eurymachus' offer to replace losses and provide further wealth draws on a recognisable alternative to continued violence. It is not merely modern commercial logic intruding into heroic anger.

His insincerity remains possible; so does the inadequacy of payment for the harms involved. What matters is that a settlement is proposed and refused. The narrative presents revenge as a chosen response, not the only imaginable one.

### Enslavement Shapes the Question of Consent

Odysseus accuses the suitors of forcing women to sleep with them; the poem elsewhere depicts some women seeking relationships with suitors. These statements need not describe exactly the same people or occasions. [Linguistic scholarship cautions against collapsing the women into one undifferentiated group](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/Dike/article/download/12251/11543/36670).

We should distinguish what the narrator represents as willing conduct from the broader lack of freedom under enslavement. Recognising that some women had desires and allegiances does not establish equal bargaining power or justify their killing.

[Wilson explains that she avoids adding sexual slurs absent from the Greek and keeps the women's enslaved status and suffering visible](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/a-translators-reckoning-with-the-women-of-the-odyssey). The interpretive consequence is substantial: a reader must confront human deaths rather than receive a verdict already packaged in abusive labels.

### Purification Does Not Mean Moral Acquittal

Fire and sulphur cleanse the house after bloodshed. Ritual purification concerns restoring a space to proper use and order; it need not mean the purifier has confessed modern legal guilt.

Conversely, completing the ritual does not settle our ethical questions. Homeric practice and modern judgement ask different things of the same smoky hall.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Keep Distinguishing People When a Group Has Wronged You

Telemachus' intervention saves lives because he knows particular histories. Collective labels make decisions fast; specific knowledge can make them fairer.

### Admit the Error Before It Finds a Scapegoat

The open storeroom could have confirmed a suspicion against the women. Telemachus' admission restores the actual chain of events. Responsibility is especially valuable when someone else is easier to blame.

### Separate Stopping Harm From Adding Humiliation

Restraint of an attacker and torment of a captive are different acts. The scene makes us ask when the aim has shifted from preventing injury to making someone suffer.

### Make Room for a Dissenting Witness

Phemius survives because Telemachus can interrupt his father. Decision-making becomes dangerous when authority cannot be halted by someone who knows more about a particular case.

### Let Relief Remain Compatible With Grief

The survivors have reasons to welcome Odysseus. That relief need not require the reader to forget the condemned. More than one truthful response can belong to the same ending.

## Stoic Dialogue

### Seneca and the Desire to Punish

[Seneca's *On Anger*, Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_I), distinguishes rational correction from the passion that wants to repay pain with pain. Odysseus' refusal of every payment and his command to prolong Melanthius' suffering make that distinction urgent.

The overlap is a concern with wrongful conduct; the disagreement concerns whether suffering is itself a satisfying objective. Seneca's position is not that society must tolerate every injury. It is that anger is an unreliable guide to the response.

For a modern reader, the useful question is what remains necessary once immediate danger has ended. A reason to restrain does not automatically become a reason to degrade.

### Human Worth Beneath Household Status

In [Letter 47](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_47), Seneca urges masters to regard enslaved people as human beings and to judge character rather than occupation. The sympathetic bird simile for the women makes a related claim upon perception: the condemned remain creatures seeking life and shelter.

Seneca still writes within a slaveholding order. His counsel challenges contempt without establishing modern equality. Like Homer's pathos, it prompts a question about sympathy: how far can it travel within accepted structures of power?

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. At what point does your emotional response to the victory change, and which detail changes it?
2. What distinguishes a self-serving plea from one that still deserves a hearing?
3. Why can Telemachus recognise compulsion in Phemius' case without exploring the women's circumstances individually?
4. How does Leiodes' death look different when you remember Book 21's description of him?
5. When does loyalty to people we love narrow our concern for everyone else?
6. What is the difference between stopping harm, imposing punishment, and insisting on humiliation?
7. Can ritual, procedure, or a return to routine make an unresolved moral question feel settled?
8. What would it mean to honour the survivors' relief without asking the dead to disappear from the story?

## Connections

### Book 9: The Meaning of Being Trapped

In the Cyclops' cave, Odysseus survived partly by controlling access and timing. Now he commands the doorway while other people search for escape.

The situations are not morally identical, but the reversal changes our vantage point. The hero's remembered helplessness does not prevent him from producing helplessness in others.

### Books 17–20: Tests Become Judgements

Earlier begging scenes differentiated generosity, contempt, hesitation, and complicity. Book 22 makes those distinctions consequential, but not evenly. Some receive individual consideration; others disappear into collective punishment.

The series' recurring question about hospitality has become a question about the limits of the household's authority over the people inside it.

### Margaret Atwood's *The Penelopiad*

[Atwood's retelling gives Penelope and the twelve hanged women a renewed claim to tell the story](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/652902/penelope-y-las-doce-criadas--the-penelopiad-by-margaret-atwood/). Its central challenge grows directly from this Book: who gets to explain the deaths after the victorious narrative moves on?

A retelling is an interpretation, not missing evidence about Homer's characters. Its value is in making a narrative silence impossible to overlook.

### The Revenge Story's Difficult Ending

Many later revenge dramas and thrillers build desire for a confrontation, then test the audience by continuing after the antagonist falls. The relation here is a comparison of narrative form rather than a claim about one line of influence.

Book 22's extended aftermath shows why the extra scenes matter. The fulfilment of suspense is not the same as the completion of moral thought.

## Memorable Passage

> “Their feet moved convulsively for a while, but not for very long.”

— Samuel Butler translation, Book 22

The sentence follows the hanging of the twelve women. Its brevity does not make the deaths small. It forces attention onto bodies struggling where the narrative offers no individual defence or farewell.

The final qualification can sound chillingly dismissive, or painfully exact about how quickly lives end. That unresolved tone is part of its force. The sentence denies the reader a wholly abstract category such as punishment or purification. Whatever judgement has been announced, these are living bodies made to stop moving.

## Worth Arguing With

Odysseus tells Eurycleia not to boast over dead men. That rule can express reverence for mortality and recognition that victory depends on the gods. It also stands beside his companions' taunts and the deliberate degradation of captives.

Does solemnity make violence just? A refusal to celebrate may constrain pride while leaving the punishment itself unexamined. The contrast asks us to distinguish the manners of power from its moral limits.

There is a second temptation: to assume that because the poem supports the restoration of Odysseus, every image must support every killing. [Gregory Nagy's commentary explicitly acknowledges the moral difficulty of the extreme retribution](https://classical-inquiries.chs.harvard.edu/a-sampling-of-comments-on-odyssey-rhapsody-22/). Homer can align the main action with divine victory while giving the victims vulnerability and pathos. Neither a wholly approving nor a wholly detached reading accounts for everything on the page.

The most demanding response is to retain the distinctions the action sometimes overrides: armed threat and captivity, deliberate cruelty and coerced service, a claim of guilt and an opportunity to answer it.

## Final Reflection

Book 22 ends with people touching a man they have missed. That tenderness is not a mistake in the narrative. Neither are the bodies, the pleas cut short, or the last movements of the women. Homer has placed them in the same house and asked the reader to remain there long enough to see them together.

The suitors have caused profound harm. Ending their domination brings real relief. But a justified desire for deliverance cannot answer every question about the means and extent of revenge. The Book makes those questions concrete by showing decisions that could have gone differently: a witness interrupts, a singer is spared, a captive is tormented, a sentence is made more degrading.

Perhaps its enduring lesson is that judgement becomes most necessary at the moment victory makes it seem unnecessary. Success can persuade people that their cause has explained everything. The vulnerable people still in their power show what remains unexplained.

We need not deny the affection of the final embrace to hear the silence around it. We can recognise what Odysseus has recovered while asking what his recovery has cost others. A house can be cleaned, lit, and filled again with familiar voices. Whether justice has been done to everyone inside it remains a harder question.
