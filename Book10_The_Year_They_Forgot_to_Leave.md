# Book 10 – The Year They Forgot to Leave

## What Happened?

Odysseus’ remaining fleet reaches Aeolia, where Aeolus hosts him for a month and gives him a sealed ox-hide bag containing every adverse wind. With only the West Wind released, Ithaca comes into view after nine days. Odysseus, who has steered without rest, falls asleep. His men assume the bag conceals treasure that their captain is keeping for himself. They open it, and the freed winds drive the ships back to Aeolus. Concluding that the gods must hate Odysseus, Aeolus refuses further help.

After six days of rowing, the fleet enters the enclosed harbour of the Laestrygonians. Odysseus alone moors his ship outside. Giant inhabitants attack, spear the trapped crews like fish, and destroy every ship except his.

The survivors reach Aeaea, Circe’s island. Odysseus divides them into two groups; the lot sends Eurylochus and twenty-two men inland. Circe welcomes them with food and wine, drugs them, and turns them into pigs while leaving their human minds intact. Only the suspicious Eurylochus remains outside and returns to report the disaster.

Odysseus goes alone to rescue them. Hermes meets him, supplies a protective herb called *moly*, and tells him how to compel Circe to swear she will not harm him. Her drug fails; he draws his sword; she identifies him and invites him to bed. He agrees only after she swears not to harm him. Odysseus then refuses food until his companions are restored. Circe makes them human again, younger, taller, and better-looking than before, and welcomes the entire crew.

They remain in comfort for a year until the men remind Odysseus of home. Circe then reveals that the route to Ithaca first leads to the dead: Odysseus must consult the prophet Tiresias in Hades. As the crew prepares to leave, the drunken Elpenor wakes on Circe’s roof, forgets the stairs, falls, and dies unnoticed. The Book ends not with a voyage home but with men weeping beside a ship bound for the underworld.

## Why This Book Exists

Book 10 dismantles the fantasy that intelligence alone makes a good leader. Odysseus can outthink a Cyclops, resist a goddess’s drug, and rescue transformed men. Yet he cannot maintain trust around a sealed bag, delegate the helm, hear dissent without fury, or remember the shared mission until his crew prompts him. His competence is real; its limits are relational.

The Book repeatedly gives Odysseus apparent control, then shows what control excludes. Aeolus seals the winds. Odysseus grips the rudder for nine days. The ships pack tightly into a protected harbour. Circe domesticates wild animals and alters human bodies. Every perfect enclosure becomes dangerous: the bag bursts, the harbour turns into a killing jar, the palace becomes a pigsty, and a year of shelter becomes delay. Safety without openness can become another form of captivity.

The adventures also redistribute moral intelligence. In Book 9, the famous hero ignored his crew’s good advice at the Cyclops’ cave. In Book 10, Eurylochus survives because he distrusts a beautiful welcome, then invokes the Cyclops disaster when others prepare to follow Odysseus back to Circe. Odysseus responds by considering decapitation. Homer lets the cautious subordinate be both irritating and correct.

Circe’s transformation is the Book’s deeper structural surprise. She begins as a predatory host who erases autonomy; she ends as healer, lover, host, and indispensable guide. Odysseus’ men are restored physically, then spend a year recovering psychologically. [Thomas Van Nortwick’s Book 10 commentary](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/intro/essays-book-10) notices that Circe names what the adventure story usually hides: the travellers have been depleted in body and spirit by sustained danger.

But restoration creates its own risk. The lotus destroyed the desire for home through magic. On Aeaea, after the magic is reversed, Odysseus and his men still remain for twelve months. Book 10 exists to show that a journey may be threatened by terror, mistrust, exhausted control, and finally by the reasonable wish to stop struggling. The hardest delay to judge is the one that looks like healing.

## Major Themes

### 1. Control Is Not the Same as Leadership

The wind bag seems to solve the problem of uncertainty. Every hostile force is contained; one favourable wind remains; home is visible. Yet the solution depends on one exhausted man guarding both helm and secret.

Odysseus never tells the crew what the bag contains. They fill the silence with a plausible grievance: he repeatedly receives honours and gifts while they return with empty hands. Their envy is destructive, but it does not arise from nowhere. A leader who hoards information may mistake obedience for trust until curiosity finds the knot.

This develops Book 9’s lesson at the cave. There Odysseus ignored good advice because he wanted an encounter. Here he refuses relief because he wants total control. In both cases the group pays for a strength extended beyond wisdom. [Dickinson’s commentary likewise identifies control as the connective tissue of the Aeolus episode](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/intro/essays-book-10).

### 2. Suspicion Can Be a Form of Care

Polites hears Circe’s beautiful singing and urges contact. Eurylochus stays outside because he suspects a trap. His caution preserves the only witness capable of reporting what happened.

When he later opposes returning, the poem encourages impatience with him: Odysseus has already succeeded, and the restored men are safe. Yet Eurylochus does not know about Hermes, *moly*, the oath, or Circe’s reversal. He reasons from the evidence available—Polyphemus, vanished companions, an enchantress’s house—and reaches a defensible conclusion.

Book 6 praised Odysseus for approaching Nausicaa without triggering fear. Book 10 asks readers to honour fear when danger is real. Courage initiates rescue; caution preserves information. A functioning group needs both, and the person who warns “not yet” should not have to be infallible to remain valuable.

### 3. Human Form Does Not Guarantee Human Freedom

Circe gives the men pigs’ bodies while their minds remain human. The punishment is therefore not loss of consciousness but consciousness trapped inside an identity others can treat as livestock. They know what has happened and cannot make themselves recognised.

The episode complicates the common reading that Circe merely reveals their greed. Homer never says the men become pigs because they eat like pigs. Their trust in hospitality is exploited through a drug meant to make them forget home. Transformation is an act of domination before it is a moral allegory.

The inverse is equally unsettling. Once restored to handsome human bodies, they remain dependent on Circe’s household and drift for a year. Freedom requires more than looking human or escaping a pen. It requires memory, agency, and a future one is still choosing.

### 4. Hospitality Can Be Broken—and Repaired

Aeolus receives Odysseus generously, then expels him as contaminated by divine hatred. The Laestrygonians convert a harbour into a slaughterhouse. Circe imitates hospitality—door opened, seats offered, food served—in order to enslave her guests.

Yet Circe later performs the rituals genuinely. She restores bodies, reunites companions, orders baths and clean clothes, serves food, recognises trauma, and eventually helps them leave. Book 9 made host and guest morally unstable categories. Book 10 shows hospitality itself changing character when coercion is replaced by oath, restitution, and care.

The repair is imperfect: violence and sexual bargaining precede it, and the men remain in a goddess’s power. Still, Homer imagines that someone who violated relationship can become its protector. Moral identity is not fixed at the first threshold.

### 5. Rest Can Restore Purpose—or Replace It

Circe correctly observes that the survivors are exhausted and joyless. Their feasting is not immediately decadence; bodies and minds cannot sustain endless emergency. The year on Aeaea gives them safety, food, sleep, companionship, and release from vigilance.

But no one marks when recovery becomes postponement. Odysseus, who declared Ithaca dearer than any foreign luxury, needs his men to tell him it is time to think of home. The crew who opened the wind bag now safeguard the mission their leader has allowed to fade.

This is a subtler danger than the lotus. There the wish for home vanished chemically and instantly. Here worthy needs—rest, pleasure, intimacy—quietly occupy all available time. Homer asks not whether we deserve rest, but how we know when rest has completed its work.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Sealed Bag

The bag turns invisible forces into a guarded object. It symbolises the dream that uncertainty can be contained once and for all.

Its silver binding is technically perfect; the social arrangement around it is not. The crew see concealment and imagine inequality. The knot holds against wind but not against mistrust. Systems fail at their human seam.

### Ithaca’s Fires

The sailors come close enough to see people tending fires on Ithaca. Home is no longer an abstraction; it is ordinary labour and warmth visible across the water.

That closeness intensifies the failure. Disaster does not occur because the destination is remote but because arrival relaxes vigilance and awakens comparison. Sometimes the final mile is dangerous precisely because success feels already possessed.

### The Enclosed Harbour

The Laestrygonian harbour appears ideal: steep cliffs stop the wind and a narrow entrance protects the water. Every captain except Odysseus brings his ship inside.

What protects against nature creates total exposure to an enemy. The ships cannot manoeuvre and are crushed against one another. Odysseus’ less comfortable anchorage preserves escape. Resilience often requires unused space, imperfect efficiency, and a route no one needs until everything changes.

### Smoke

Smoke usually signals habitation, food, and the possibility of welcome. At both Laestrygonia and Aeaea, Odysseus sees smoke before people and must decide whether to investigate.

The sign of civilisation no longer guarantees humane conduct. Fire tells him that intelligence is present, not what that intelligence serves.

### Circe’s Loom and Song

Circe sings while weaving, echoing Calypso and the domestic artistry associated with powerful women throughout the poem. The beautiful sound makes the hidden worker seem approachable.

Weaving produces ordered surfaces from crossing threads; Circe similarly reorganises appearances. Her house is a crafted world in which beasts behave like pets and men wear beasts’ bodies. Beauty here is neither innocent nor false. It is a power that can conceal intention.

### The Wand and the Sword

Circe’s wand changes bodies; Odysseus’ sword threatens bodily destruction. Their confrontation stages two kinds of coercive power, magical and martial.

Hermes’ instructions allow the sword to interrupt the wand, but not to create trust. That requires an oath. The sequence matters: force can stop immediate domination; only a binding promise makes shared vulnerability possible.

### *Moly*

The protective plant has a black root and a milk-white flower. Mortals find it almost impossible to uproot; a god gives it freely.

Its contrasting colours suit a Book in which appearances reverse: poison resembles hospitality, a witch becomes a guide, animals contain human minds, and comfort points towards danger. *Moly* also prevents hero worship. Odysseus’ resistance is not innate superiority; courage succeeds because help arrives first.

### Elpenor’s Roof

The roof offers cool distance from the communal feast. It becomes a lethal threshold between the protected house and the hard ground.

Elpenor survives war, cannibals, storm, and enchantment, then dies because sleep, drink, and disorientation make him forget the stairs. Epic danger is not always magnificent. A body can fall out of the story while everyone is busy preparing for the next great task.

## Character Insights

### Odysseus

Odysseus displays three kinds of leadership: control, rescue, and care. He grips the helm until exhaustion, keeps his ship outside the fatal harbour, walks alone towards Circe, and refuses to eat until his companions are human again.

His loyalty is therefore undeniable. So is his volatility. When Eurylochus warns the crew, Odysseus’ first thought is to cut off the head of his own relative. The hero who resisted the immediate impulse to kill Polyphemus inside the cave nearly murders the man whose caution saved half the crew.

Most revealingly, he reports that the men must remind him to leave Circe. His narrative often blames their folly, but here he preserves evidence that they can carry purpose when he does not. His greatness includes the strange capacity to tell against himself.

### Circe

Circe is Odysseus’ most exact counterpart so far. Both are resourceful, suspicious of strangers, capable of disguise and coercion, skilled at manipulating appetite, and eventually capable of protecting those they once endangered. An [OCR guide to the Greek text](https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/317853-the-odyssey.pdf) notes that she alone in the poem shares the special language of inexhaustible resourcefulness associated with Odysseus.

Her change after the oath is abrupt but substantial. She does not merely undo the spell. She makes the men stronger, creates a place of recovery, understands their depleted minds, and gives precise instructions for a journey no mortal guide could lead.

She cannot be reduced either to redeemed helper or wicked witch. She violates autonomy, then becomes the person best able to restore and direct it.

### Eurylochus

Eurylochus embodies intelligence without heroic appetite. He stays outside the inviting house, watches long enough to know no one returns, overcomes shock, and brings the news back. Without him there is no rescue.

His second refusal is not cowardice in any simple sense. Odysseus asks him to revisit the site where twenty-two men disappeared; later, he asks the whole crew to trust the person who transformed them. Eurylochus has reasons.

The [Dickinson commentary calls him a possible double of Odysseus](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/intro/essays-book-10): the version of the leader who might have explored less and brought more men home. Caution alone might never complete the journey, but the poem’s admiration for boldness should not make us forget whom boldness has buried.

### The Crew

The crew’s worst action grows from resentment. They believe Odysseus has received another private share of wealth after everyone endured the same journey. Instead of asking, they conspire; instead of explaining, he sleeps beside a secret.

Butler sometimes adds a patronising note not demanded by the Greek, calling the weeping survivors “silly” when they welcome Odysseus back. Wilson’s plainer treatment matters: their tears belong to men who have just lost nearly their entire community, not comic dependants awaiting their superior.

### Aeolus

Aeolus first appears as an ideal host and precise steward. He listens to Odysseus for a month, arranges the winds, and creates a near-perfect escort home.

On the second arrival, he interprets failure as proof of divine rejection. The reasoning protects his worldview: if his gift was good and the gods favour order, renewed suffering must reveal something corrupt in the sufferer. His hospitality ends exactly when the guest’s need becomes difficult to explain.

### Hermes

Hermes does not perform the rescue for Odysseus. He supplies the otherwise inaccessible herb, explains Circe’s sequence of moves, and leaves the mortal to act. His help is practical rather than spectacular.

The encounter corrects the image of the self-made hero. Odysseus’ courage is necessary, but without divine knowledge it would deliver another man into the pigsty. Capability includes receiving help one could never have produced alone.

### Elpenor

Odysseus introduces Elpenor through negation: neither notably brave nor especially intelligent. He receives a name only at the moment he exits the living company.

His death strips epic of heroic consolation. No enemy defeats him, no god targets him, and no comrades witness him. He falls because he is drunk, startled, and forgetful. Homer makes an undistinguished man the threshold figure for a journey to the dead, reminding us that mortality does not rank its victims by narrative importance.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Winds, Gods, and Seamanship

For ancient sailors, wind was both a physical necessity and a divine agency beyond dependable control. Aeolus’ bag gives folktale form to the sailor’s impossible wish: favourable conditions secured for the entire passage.

The wonder remains attached to real seamanship. Odysseus holds the steering oar, the crew row when wind fails, and anchorage determines survival. Supernatural fortune never removes the need for judgement.

### Misfortune as Moral Evidence

Aeolus assumes that repeated disaster proves Odysseus is hated by the gods. The [Cambridge narratological commentary](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511482137.013) identifies this reversal: a man described as dear to the gods rejects a guest whom he reads as divinely detested.

Ancient epic often treats prosperity and divine favour as connected, but it also shows how unreliable human interpretation can be. Aeolus knows the fleet returned; he does not know why. Theology fills the gap left by missing information.

### The Autonomous Female Household

Circe possesses land, servants, craft, food, sexuality, knowledge, and supernatural force without father, husband, or civic assembly supervising her. For an audience shaped by male-controlled households, that independence could register as both alluring and threatening.

The plot converts anxiety into bodily terms: men fear being reduced to animals or rendered sexually powerless. Yet Circe is not simply disorder. Her home is highly organised, and once bound by oath she performs hospitality more completely than many male hosts.

### Oaths and Unequal Consent

A solemn oath calls the gods to punish false speech. Odysseus requires one before making himself sexually vulnerable because Hermes warns that Circe may use nakedness to strip his power.

The oath establishes a minimum of safety, not modern equality. Circe first imprisons his men; Odysseus threatens her with a sword; sex follows within that field of pressure. Ancient narrative can present the resulting alliance without giving modern readers a reason to call the encounter uncomplicated consent.

### Drugs and *Pharmaka*

The Greek idea of a *pharmakon* can encompass medicine, poison, and magical drug. Circe uses one mixture to dominate and another to restore; Hermes counters drug with herb. Substance is not morally stable. Its meaning depends on knowledge, dose, purpose, and power over the recipient.

### Descent to the Dead

A heroic journey to the underworld is called a *katabasis*, literally a going down. Such descents bring knowledge unavailable among the living. Circe’s instructions combine geography, sacrifice, libations, prayer, and control of access to the blood that lets the dead communicate.

The paradox is structural: to move towards home, Odysseus must travel in the opposite direction from ordinary life. Progress sometimes requires entering what the destination was meant to escape.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Explain the Bag

Information asymmetry invites stories about unfairness. If people must protect a plan whose logic they are forbidden to know, suspicion is not an accidental complication; it is part of the design.

### Do Not Build a System with One Wakeful Person as Its Safeguard

Odysseus’ nine-day vigil looks heroic until biology defeats it. A process that collapses when its best person sleeps is already broken.

### Preserve the Uncomfortable Dissenter

Eurylochus is frightened, repetitive, and essential. A warning system that functions only when the messenger is likeable will fail precisely when stress makes everyone less likeable.

### Leave Room to Escape

The crowded harbour maximises shelter and destroys optionality. In organisations, finances, schedules, and relationships, spare capacity can look wasteful until it becomes the only route out.

### Rescue Before Reward

Odysseus will not eat while his companions remain pigs. Celebration before restoration would allow personal success to disguise collective failure.

### Give Recovery an Exit Condition

Rest should restore the ability to choose and act. Without a moment for reassessment, refuge can quietly inherit the permanence of a destination.

### Do Not Treat Suffering as Proof of Bad Character

Aeolus turns unexplained misfortune into moral diagnosis. People in pain often need curiosity before judgement—especially when the observer possesses only the outcome.

## Stoic Dialogue

### The Helm and the Limits of Control

**Homeric passage:** Odysseus steers for nine days without handing over the rudder, falls asleep within sight of Ithaca, and wakes to disaster.

**Stoic idea:** Stoicism distinguishes what depends on our judgement and action from outcomes that do not. It does not promise control over wind, other people, or sleep.

**Agreement:** Odysseus’ duty is to steer wisely, not to guarantee the universe. His endurance matters, but no effort can convert an external outcome into absolute possession.

**Difference:** A Stoic would not equate relinquishing control with passivity. Odysseus acts as though responsibility requires personal command of every variable. His refusal to delegate is attachment to an outcome disguised as diligence.

**Modern reflection:** Ask whether your vigilance serves the task or reassures you that nothing important can happen without you.

### Human Minds in Animal Bodies

**Homeric passage:** Circe gives the sailors pigs’ bodies and voices, but their awareness remains unchanged.

**Stoic idea:** For the Stoics, moral agency lies in the rational faculty, not in beauty, status, health, or how others classify the body.

**Agreement:** The men’s human worth survives visible degradation. Circe can alter appearance and social treatment without making their inner awareness porcine.

**Difference:** Stoic inward freedom can sound insufficient when a person is caged, voiceless, and fed as livestock. Preserved consciousness does not cancel material domination; it can make domination more painful.

**Modern reflection:** Dignity may be inalienable, but justice still requires changing the conditions that deny its recognition.

### Odysseus’ Anger at Eurylochus

**Homeric passage:** Hearing Eurylochus urge caution, Odysseus considers killing him before the crew intervenes.

**Stoic idea:** Seneca treats anger not as useful courage but as a judgement that retaliation is appropriate, a judgement capable of outrunning reason.

**Agreement:** The crew’s gentle interruption does what Odysseus cannot do alone: create time between impulse and action. Anger feels decisive while nearly destroying a valuable truth-teller.

**Difference:** Homer does not pause to condemn the thought severely. The heroic hierarchy makes fierce enforcement of authority intelligible, even when the contemplated punishment is wildly disproportionate.

**Modern reflection:** The moment a leader most wants to silence resistance may be the moment the group most needs resistance to remain audible.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. What important “bag” are you asking others to protect without explaining what it contains?
2. Where has taking full responsibility become a refusal to trust or delegate?
3. Who in your life sees danger early but communicates it inconveniently?
4. What apparently safe harbour leaves you no room to manoeuvre?
5. When has necessary recovery quietly become avoidance?
6. Which external label has failed to alter what you know yourself to be?
7. When do you infer character from someone else’s good or bad fortune?
8. What ordinary risk are you neglecting because your attention is fixed on dramatic ones?

## Connections

### Virgil’s Keeper of the Winds

In Book 1 of the *Aeneid*, Virgil reworks Aeolus into a subordinate power whom Juno persuades to unleash a storm against Aeneas. Homer’s Aeolus contains destructive winds to help a traveller; Virgil’s releases them to obstruct one. Both versions imagine political order as management of forces that remain dangerous even when confined.

### The *Epic of Gilgamesh* and the Host at the Edge

Near the Waters of Death, Gilgamesh meets Siduri, a woman associated with food, drink, and counsel at the boundary of the known world. Circe occupies a similar narrative position: dangerous at first, then restorative, she feeds an exhausted traveller and tells him how to approach death’s territory.

The connection helps us see her as more than temptress. Boundary women in ancient journey stories often possess the practical knowledge heroic force lacks.

### Plutarch’s *Gryllus*

In a later comic-philosophical dialogue, Plutarch imagines Odysseus asking Circe to restore transformed Greeks. One of them, now an animal, refuses: beasts may live more naturally and virtuously than humans. The reversal argues with Homer’s assumption that restoration to human shape is self-evidently desirable.

It also exposes Book 10’s unanswered question. If the men retain their minds, what do they experience in animal form—and would every transformed person define liberation exactly as Odysseus does?

### Madeline Miller’s *Circe*

Miller’s novel retells the myth from Circe’s perspective, giving long interior history to a figure who enters Odysseus’ narration as a danger, lover, and helper. The shift of narrator changes the moral geometry: the armed men are no longer simply explorers arriving at an enchantress’s stage.

That modern retelling extends a question Homer already permits. What happens to our judgement when the person with transformative power also gains the power to tell the story?

### *Spirited Away*

Hayao Miyazaki’s film transforms a child’s parents into pigs after they consume food that is not theirs. Its young heroine must navigate a magical female-ruled workplace, remember her name, and rescue people whose human identities have become invisible.

The parallels are not one-to-one, but both stories link appetite, altered bodies, memory, labour, and the difficult preservation of self inside an enchanted household.

## Memorable Passage

> “Sir, it is time you began to think about going home, if so be you are to be spared to see your house and native country at all.”
> — Samuel Butler translation

The line is spoken not by a god, king, or prophet, but by Odysseus’ crew. Its quietness is the point. No spell is broken and no monster defeated; the men simply restore the destination to their leader’s attention.

Their intervention revises the hierarchy of wisdom established by Odysseus’ own narrative. He has repeatedly described the crew’s folly, and with reason. They opened the wind bag. Yet they now remember what the man famous for longing has stopped acting upon.

The phrasing also contains a gentle rebuke. It is not merely time to go home; it is time for Odysseus to begin *thinking* about home. The failure precedes action. Purpose has faded from active consciousness while feasts repeat and months turn.

Circe’s hospitality has repaired them enough to leave, but comfort supplies no natural alarm announcing that recovery is complete. The crew must create that alarm together. Sometimes the people we lead do not distract us from our calling. They return it to us.

## Worth Arguing With

### Is the Wind-Bag Disaster Only the Crew’s Folly?

Odysseus frames the disaster as his men’s stupidity, and opening a mysterious object at sea without permission is plainly reckless. But his account reveals organisational failure as well. He keeps the bag’s contents secret, monopolises the helm until collapse, and presides over a distribution of honour the men experience as unfair.

Their resentment does not justify sabotage. His excellence does not absolve him from creating the conditions in which resentment becomes catastrophic. Blame can be shared without being equal.

### Should Circe and Odysseus Be Read as a Romance?

The poem moves rapidly from magical assault and sword threat to oath and bed. Ancient epic is comfortable presenting the outcome as alliance. A modern reader should resist smoothing that sequence into uncomplicated seduction.

Circe holds the men’s bodies and future; Odysseus approaches with divine protection she cannot overcome and a drawn weapon she believes he will use. Both negotiate under coercive pressure. The relationship later acquires care, pleasure, and trust, but later tenderness cannot retroactively make the beginning free.

### Do the Pigs Reveal the Men’s “True Nature”?

Later readers often moralise the transformation: greedy men become the animals they already resemble. It is an elegant interpretation, but Homer does not state it. The men accept what appears to be proper hospitality after a dangerous journey. Circe drugs them deliberately.

Calling domination a revelation of the victim’s essence risks making power look like truth. The more textually grounded horror is that the men remain inwardly human while their imposed bodies determine how the world may use them.

## Final Reflection

Book 10 asks what happens after endurance succeeds.

Odysseus and his men have survived war, storm, cannibals, and the collapse of almost their entire fleet. Circe sees what heroic narration can overlook: they are not merely alive; they are depleted. They need food, bathing, sleep, companionship, and time in which nothing attacks them. Recovery is part of the journey, not a betrayal of it.

Yet healing has no automatic ending. The months circle quietly until the crew tells its captain to think about home. The men he calls foolish become guardians of purpose; the leader who can resist magic cannot notice that comfort has begun to choose for him.

This is Book 10’s mature understanding of agency. We can be trapped by force, as the ships are trapped in the harbour and the men in pigs’ bodies. We can also become stationary inside things that are genuinely good. Rest, love, safety, and pleasure do not become evil when they delay us. They become incomplete goods mistaken for the whole life.

Perhaps Homer’s lesson is that purpose must be remembered collectively. No one can steer for nine days forever. We need people who can take the helm, question the sealed bag, interrupt our anger, and tell us—gently, inconveniently—when it is time to leave.
