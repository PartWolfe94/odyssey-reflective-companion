# Book 19 – The Scar and the Story

## What Happened?

After the suitors leave, Odysseus and Telemachus remove the weapons from the hall. Athena lights their work with a golden lamp. Telemachus goes to bed, leaving his disguised father to meet Penelope.

Melantho again insults the stranger, but Penelope rebukes her and has a comfortable seat brought for him. Odysseus praises Penelope as though she were a just king. She describes the pressure to remarry and recounts her weaving trick, now defeated. Asked about his origins, he invents another Cretan identity, calling himself Aethon and claiming to have entertained Odysseus before Troy.

Penelope tests him by asking about her husband's clothes and companions. His accurate description of garments she supplied convinces her that the stranger knew Odysseus. She weeps while he conceals his own emotion. He promises that her husband is alive and will return soon, but she remains doubtful.

Penelope offers him hospitality, including a footbath. Odysseus accepts washing only from an older woman who knows suffering. Eurycleia notices his resemblance to her master, then recognises the scar above his knee. The narrative pauses to explain his naming by his grandfather Autolycus and the boar hunt that wounded him. Eurycleia drops his foot into the basin and tries to alert Penelope, whose attention Athena diverts. Odysseus grips the nurse's throat and threatens her into secrecy.

Penelope resumes their conversation. She describes sleepless indecision and a dream in which an eagle kills her beloved geese, then identifies itself as her returning husband and the geese as the suitors. Odysseus endorses the interpretation. Penelope questions the reliability of dreams and announces a contest: she will marry whoever can string Odysseus' bow and shoot through twelve axes. He encourages her to proceed. She withdraws upstairs, mourns, and finally sleeps.

## Why This Book Exists

After so much travelling, the poem makes its most difficult encounter a conversation between two people sitting near a fire. Odysseus can reach Penelope's chair more easily than he can reach a shared understanding with her.

The Book places three ways of knowing beside one another. A story supplies credible information. A scar establishes bodily identity. A dream offers a meaning for the future. None does everything. Penelope correctly verifies that her visitor possesses intimate knowledge, but that does not tell her how he obtained it. Eurycleia discovers the man, but cannot make her knowledge public. The dream supplies its own explanation, yet Penelope still doubts it.

This is also the first sustained opportunity for Penelope to describe her predicament to Odysseus. In Book 18 he watched her act; here he hears what action has cost. The weaving story, previously reported by Antinous, becomes the account of a woman whose available strategies are running out.

The scar's history interrupts suspense to take us behind Troy. Before the famous survivor, there was a child other people named and a wounded boy other people helped recover.

By the end, a private conversation has established the contest Penelope intends to hold. The Book cannot be skipped because homecoming must acquire a human history before it can become a public fact. Odysseus has returned to his house. The people in it still have to discover what his presence means.

## Major Themes

### 1. Accurate Evidence Can Support an Incomplete Conclusion

Penelope asks about clothes and companions rather than accepting a moving story. Odysseus answers with details only someone close to her husband should know. Her method is sensible; her conclusion remains incomplete.

Book 16 showed Telemachus misreading a transformed body. Here ordinary evidence is more persuasive than a miracle, but still cannot identify its source. The stranger knows the clothes because he wore them.

The modern question is practical: does a convincing detail prove the claim being made, or merely prove access to information? A fraud, an eyewitness, and the person concerned may possess the same fact. Penelope's intelligence appears in her testing, not in immunity to deception.

### 2. Waiting Is Work With an Expiry Date

Penelope's weaving bought time by turning a duty to the family into an obstacle to remarriage. Now the trick is exposed, her parents press her, and Telemachus resents the estate's consumption. Continuing to wait has become a decision with costs for other people.

In Book 2 an aggrieved suitor described the weaving. Penelope's account restores the nightly labour behind the delay.

How do we distinguish steadfastness from being trapped? Her announcement of the bow contest makes that question urgent. Loyalty cannot always preserve the conditions that once made loyalty possible; sometimes it must choose under intolerable uncertainty.

### 3. Intimacy and Control Can Occupy the Same Gesture

Odysseus feels compassion while his invented account makes Penelope cry. He chooses concealment despite that compassion. When Eurycleia recognises him, his hand at her throat turns a moment of affection into an order.

The secrecy that joined father and son in Book 16 now exposes its unequal distribution. Odysseus decides who may know, speak, and be reassured. His tactical reasons are serious, but so are the emotional costs borne by others.

When does protecting someone become deciding for them? The question applies wherever one person controls information about a situation both must live through. Loving motives do not make the distribution of power disappear.

### 4. Identity Includes What You Did Not Choose

Odysseus invents names throughout his travels. Here the narrative gives us a name bestowed in infancy and a scar received despite his skill. Neither originated as a deliberate self-description.

Autolycus connects the name to anger or hostility; the young hunter is wounded before killing the boar. Cleverness, inherited conflict, courage, and vulnerability enter the same biography. The body carries evidence that the storyteller cannot revise.

After the invented histories of Books 14 and 17, this involuntary history matters. How much of a person can be understood from what they choose to tell? Sometimes care, memory, and bodily experience preserve a continuity that public achievement obscures.

### 5. A True Prediction May Not Exhaust a Dream

The eagle explains that it represents Odysseus and the geese represent the suitors. Odysseus says the interpretation is settled. Penelope nevertheless grieves for the geese and questions whether the dream will come true.

Earlier bird omens, including Helen's in Book 15, seemed to translate predation into confident promises of return. Here the person dreaming is emotionally attached to the creatures killed. Desire and dread coexist within the same image.

Does she fear the violence, mourn a familiar world, feel ambivalent about the suitors, or test her visitor through the story? [Comparative scholarship also places the dream within traditions of women dreaming before a beloved's return](https://classics-at.chs.harvard.edu/classics14-levaniouk/). Several readings remain possible. An explanation may fit a symbol while leaving the dreamer's feelings unexplained. Listening requires room for both.

## Symbols and Motifs

### The Lamp and the Hidden Weapons

Athena illuminates the hall while father and son remove its arms. Telemachus sees the extraordinary light but receives an instruction to stop asking questions.

Book 16's plan becomes action. Light assists concealment: seeing more does not mean being told more.

### The Web Retold

The shroud for Laertes is both an actual textile and a way of managing time. Its unfinished state once postponed a future Penelope could not accept.

Now she tells its history herself. The change of narrator matters as much as the object: a suitor's grievance becomes an account of ingenuity under pressure. Even familiar events alter when the person doing the work speaks.

### Snow and Unmoving Eyes

Penelope's grief is compared to mountain snow thawing into rivers; Odysseus keeps his eyes hard and still. Water has carried his vulnerability throughout the sea journey. Now she dissolves while he must remain outwardly solid.

[Wilson explains that her translation preserves the disturbing sense of Penelope's flesh and cheeks melting, rather than reducing the image to flowing tears](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/a-translators-reckoning-with-the-women-of-the-odyssey). Her reading makes the comparison about erosion of self as well as beautiful sorrow. This differs materially from Butler's emphasis on an overflowing face and melted heart.

### The Brooch's Dog and Fawn

The gold brooch depicts a dog holding a struggling fawn. Its lifelike craft helps authenticate Odysseus' story, but its subject is predation.

After Argos' helpless welcome in Book 17, another dog appears within a token of the marriage. Here the dog is powerful and the smaller animal trapped. No single allegory is required: an ornament associated with affection also contains violence. Domestic beauty and suffering share the same surface.

### The Scar and the Spilled Basin

The scar is a healed injury that remains legible to someone who knew the injured boy. Unlike the brooch, it cannot be lent or traded.

Eurycleia's startled movement makes the basin ring and spill. Recognition briefly disrupts the orderly room. The past has entered through touch and produced a physical consequence before anyone can control its telling.

### The Nightingale

Penelope compares herself to the daughter of Pandareus, transformed into a bird after killing her child Itylus by mistake. This is a particular mythic version, not simply the better-known story of Philomela.

The comparison makes maternal decision frightening. Penelope fears consequences whichever way she turns: staying consumes her son's inheritance; leaving abandons a household she has guarded. Her song of grief carries anxiety about doing irreversible harm.

### Horn, Ivory, and the Bow

Penelope imagines two gates for dreams, distinguishing fulfilment from deception. She can name the categories without confidently placing her own dream in one.

The bow offers something different: an action that can be attempted and witnessed. Like the contests of Book 8, it joins identity to skill. Its twelve axes establish a demanding test; the precise ancient arrangement remains debated. The important transition is from interpreting a possible future to setting conditions for a decision.

## Character Insights

### Penelope

Penelope moves between scepticism, courtesy, sorrow, and decisive planning. None cancels the others. She can weep intensely and then ask a discriminating question.

Her comparison with a just king comes from Odysseus, whose praise imagines prosperity flowing from good rule. She answers by describing actual depletion and constraint. The exchange holds idealised reputation against the exhausting circumstances of sustaining it.

Her announcement of the contest should not be reduced to either surrender or a secret plan she unquestionably understands. The text gives her a decision and preserves uncertainty about the full thought behind it. That uncertainty makes her an interlocutor rather than a puzzle solved by her husband.

### Odysseus

Odysseus knows how to make a story credible, but does not perfectly control its setting. He realises the danger of the scar only after accepting the old woman's care.

His compassion for Penelope is explicit. So is his willingness to keep her grieving. Homer makes us hold affection and instrumental calculation together. The man capable of enduring pain can also decide that someone else's pain must continue for his plan to work.

His memories of garments and Eurybates reveal another side of him: identity survives in relationships, textures, and affinities, not only in heroic exploits.

### Eurycleia

Eurycleia first challenges the fairness of Zeus: how could a man so attentive to sacrifice remain lost? Loyalty does not prevent her from questioning divine treatment.

Her recognition joins perception to decades of care. She knows feet, voice, and bodily history. The supposedly subordinate person possesses knowledge the household's rulers need.

Then she promises silence and offers to distinguish loyal women from disloyal ones. Affection, pride in reliability, and participation in household discipline overlap. Her devotion is real, yet it operates within an order in which the child she nursed can threaten her life.

### Telemachus and Athena

Telemachus helps execute the weapons plan, then accepts exclusion from the adult conversation. Partnership with his father has brought responsibility without complete access.

Athena controls access more radically. She lights the removal of arms and turns Penelope's attention away from recognition. Her help explains why secrecy survives a noisy accident, while reminding us that information in this house is unequally available by divine design as well as human choice.

### Melantho and Eurynome

Melantho sees the stranger as an intrusive observer and threatens him with a firebrand. Her hostility violates the hospitality Penelope expressly wants him to receive. Odysseus answers by invoking the authority that can punish her.

Eurynome enables the encounter by bringing his seat. Domestic workers can obstruct or facilitate conversation without controlling its purpose. Criticising Melantho's insults need not make her enslavement resemble ordinary employment.

### Autolycus and the Family Before Troy

Autolycus combines family generosity with celebrated theft and false oaths, gifts associated with Hermes. Homeric excellence does not always coincide with modern moral respectability.

The hunt places Odysseus within his mother's family. His grandmother embraces him, his uncles treat the wound, and his parents hear its story on his return. The famous independent survivor was formed within repeated acts of welcome and repair.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### An Epic Household Is Not a Modern Private Home

Hospitality, inheritance, labour, marriage, and political standing converge in the same building. Penelope's remarriage would rearrange relationships far beyond a couple. Her practical constraints make sense within this structure, though understanding them need not mean endorsing them.

Homer's world also combines memories of the heroic past with later practices. We should resist treating every domestic detail as a straightforward archaeological report of Bronze Age life.

### Footwashing Is Care and Status

Washing a traveller's feet answers the bodily needs of travel and honours a guest. The work falls to a subordinate. Recognition comes through an ordinary service, not a public ceremony.

Eurycleia's intimate role as nurse does not erase enslavement, already established in Book 1. Here Odysseus' threat makes that limit to domestic affection unmistakable.

### Names Can Become Interpretations

Autolycus' explanation associates Odysseus' name with anger or hostility. Butler's wording emphasises the grandfather's anger. This is the poem making meaning through a name, not a secure modern derivation proving the hero's essential nature. A family story gives the name significance before its bearer can decide who to become.

### Dreams Belong to a Contested Field of Knowledge

Dreams can bring divine information, yet Penelope explicitly allows deception. Ancient belief here includes scepticism.

Her gates articulate the difficulty without solving it. We know more about the visitor than Penelope does, but still cannot read her mind.

## Wisdom for Modern Life

### Separate a Fact From the Claim It Supports

Penelope's question is good; the evidence has limits. When a story contains intimate, accurate details, ask which conclusion those details actually warrant. Confidence should match the strength and scope of the evidence.

### Hear the Cost Inside Someone's Competence

The weaving trick sounds ingenious until its maker describes nights spent undoing work and a future closing in. Admiring resourcefulness can make it easy to overlook the conditions requiring it.

### Remember Who Knows Your Unperformed Life

Eurycleia knows the scar because she knew the boy. People who have cared for us may hold a history absent from our carefully edited accounts of ourselves. Their knowledge deserves regard, not embarrassment.

### Leave Room for Mixed Feelings

Penelope's geese cannot be reduced to a verdict about her loyalty. Wanting a crisis resolved does not dictate how someone must feel about every part of the resolution.

## Stoic Dialogue

### Penelope and the Examination of Impressions

In [the opening of Epictetus' *Enchiridion*](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html), the reader is urged to examine appearances instead of immediately accepting them. Penelope's test of the clothing offers a related discipline: feeling moved does not end the inquiry.

The difference matters. She is seeking facts about another person's survival; Stoic practice asks especially whether a judgement about good and harm is sound. Both encourage a pause between impression and assent. Neither promises perfect information. Today the useful habit is to ask what one knows without shaming oneself for needing to know more.

### Odysseus and Eurycleia: Two Kinds of Firmness

Odysseus keeps his eyes still; Eurycleia promises the firmness of stone or iron. Their self-command serves secrecy. A Stoic would also ask whether the action served by that discipline is just.

In [*Discourses* 4.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/discourses.4.four.html), Epictetus treats avoiding quarrels and governing one's own conduct as marks of moral strength. Odysseus' threat to his nurse exposes the difference between controlling emotion and treating another person well. Calm delivery cannot make intimidation virtuous.

The modern reflection is uncomfortable but useful: composure may help us care for someone, or help us control them. We must examine its purpose.

## Questions Worth Sitting With

1. When have accurate details persuaded you of something they did not actually prove?
2. Can a relationship feel intimate while one person controls nearly all the important information?
3. What changes when Penelope tells the weaving story rather than Antinous?
4. Which parts of your history are preserved by people whose care rarely appears in your account of yourself?
5. Why does the dream retain Penelope's grief after supplying its own reassuring explanation?
6. What would count as sufficient reason to stop waiting for someone?
7. Is Eurycleia's silence freely given, compelled, or both—and what does that ambiguity do to the reunion?

## Connections

### Books 16–17: Recognition Takes Different Forms

Telemachus needs explanation, Argos responds without words, and Eurycleia recognises through touch. The sequence refuses a universal formula. People know through the relationships they have actually lived.

Penelope adds another reason to seek evidence: mistaken hope would wound deeply.

### Virgil's Gates of Sleep

Near the end of [*Aeneid* 6](https://classics.mit.edu/Virgil/aeneid.6.vi.html), Virgil reuses the gates of horn and ivory. Aeneas leaves through the gate associated with false dreams after receiving an extraordinary revelation.

The unsettling exit has prompted competing interpretations. The clear connection is that Homer supplies later epic with an image for questioning the truth-status of its own visions. A memorable symbol can carry uncertainty across centuries.

### *Othello*: The History an Object Cannot Tell

Shakespeare also makes a personal object central to a judgement about intimate life. This is a comparison, not a claim of direct borrowing from Book 19.

An object can prove that someone possesses it or knows it. It cannot automatically establish the story of how that happened. Penelope's brooch and Shakespeare's handkerchief both invite attention to the dangerous gap between evidence and explanation.

## Memorable Passage

> “Men live but for a little season; if they are hard, and deal hardly, people wish them ill so long as they are alive, and speak contemptuously of them when they are dead, but he that is righteous and deals righteously, the people tell of his praise among all lands, and many shall call him blessed.”

— Samuel Butler translation, Book 19

Penelope speaks these words while offering the stranger clean clothing, food, and rest. Reputation becomes the afterlife of treatment: what survives us is partly how it felt to be dependent on us.

The passage answers Odysseus' earlier praise of her as a king. She defines worthy authority through conduct within reach, then attempts to practise it. Mortality makes hospitality urgent. There is only a little season in which to turn kindness from an ideal into something another body can experience.

## Worth Arguing With

Does Penelope already know? Some readings interpret the dream and bow contest as covert communication with a recognised husband; others see uncertainty, intuitive affinity, or a desperate decision. The available scenes allow suggestive questions, but do not establish a secret recognition as fact. Athena's diversion of her attention at the scar explicitly preserves her lack of that particular knowledge.

Declaring that she knows everything can make her seem admirably clever at the cost of erasing the grief and risk the text gives her. Declaring her wholly passive does equal damage. Her intelligence need not become omniscience to deserve respect.

Odysseus' treatment of Eurycleia deserves a more direct challenge. Secrecy is crucial, but he makes loyalty answerable to a threat of death. The child she fed now holds her throat. The poem lets tenderness and domination meet with startling immediacy. Readers can understand the tactical danger while asking why a vulnerable person's life is treated as the security deposit for a powerful man's plan.

## Final Reflection

Book 19 brings us close enough to hear how recognition fails to be simple. A woman knows the clothes she folded. A nurse knows the leg she once saw wounded. A husband hears the life his absence has forced his wife to live. Knowledge arrives in fragments, each held by someone whose position determines what can be done with it.

The scar seems to promise certainty, and in one sense it delivers. Eurycleia has found Odysseus. Yet certainty does not produce equal speech or immediate comfort. It produces a command to remain silent. The dream seems to promise an ending, but its explanation cannot remove the dreamer's sorrow. The truth of a situation and the experience of living through it remain different things.

Perhaps the lesson is to become more patient with the kinds of knowledge people need. Facts matter. So do the histories that make facts painful, the bodies that carry what stories omit, and the unequal freedom to say what one knows.

Penelope eventually goes upstairs, leaving the conversation unfinished. For all their closeness, something still separates these two people. We want the distance closed. Homer has made us understand why closing it will require more than a name.
